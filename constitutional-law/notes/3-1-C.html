Module 03 · Fundamental Rights

Against whom Fundamental Rights are available

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 3.1 C

Against Whom Can Fundamental Rights Be Enforced? (Combined with Constitutional Remedies)

Introduction: Fundamental Rights are provided under Part III (Articles 12–35) of the Indian Constitution. They protect individual freedom, equality and dignity. These rights can generally be enforced against the State, but certain rights can also be enforced against private individuals. Articles 32 and 226 provide important remedies for their enforcement.

1. Fundamental Rights Against the State

Most Fundamental Rights protect individuals against the arbitrary actions of the State. Article 12 defines the term “State” for Part III. It includes the Government and Parliament of India, State Governments and Legislatures, local authorities and other authorities within its scope.

Public bodies and certain government-controlled organisations may also come within Article 12, depending on their functions and the degree of government control. Under Article 13, laws that take away or reduce Fundamental Rights in violation of the Constitution can be declared unconstitutional by courts.

2. Fundamental Rights Against Private Individuals

Although Fundamental Rights generally operate against the State, some rights can also be enforced against private individuals. Important examples are:

Article 15(2): It prohibits certain forms of discrimination in access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and public places on specified grounds.

Article 17: It abolishes untouchability and prohibits its practice in any form.

Article 23: It prohibits human trafficking, begar and other forms of forced labour.

Article 24: It prohibits the employment of children below fourteen years in factories, mines and other hazardous employment.

The Supreme Court has also recognised that Fundamental Rights under Articles 19 and 21 may be enforced against persons other than the State or its instrumentalities in appropriate circumstances.

3. Constitutional Remedies

Article 32: It allows a person to approach the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar considered this right essential for protecting the Constitution.

Article 226: It empowers High Courts to issue writs for enforcing Fundamental Rights and for other legal purposes.

The five important writs are:

1. Habeas Corpus: It protects a person against unlawful detention.

2. Mandamus: It directs a public authority to perform its legal duty.

3. Prohibition: It prevents a lower court or tribunal from acting beyond its legal authority.

4. Certiorari: It allows a higher court to quash an unlawful order of a lower court or tribunal.

5. Quo Warranto: It questions the legal authority of a person holding a public office.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) also allows a public-spirited person to approach the courts in appropriate cases to protect the rights of disadvantaged people or address matters of public importance.

4. Important Judicial Decisions

People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982): The Supreme Court examined the protection against forced labour under Article 23 and recognised its application to labourers employed by private contractors.

Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2023): The Supreme Court held that Fundamental Rights under Articles 19 and 21 can be enforced even against persons other than the State or its instrumentalities. It also recognised the State’s duty to protect personal liberty against threats from private actors.

Conclusion: Fundamental Rights are mainly enforceable against the State, while certain rights also apply to private individuals. Articles 32 and 226 provide constitutional remedies to protect these rights and uphold the rule of law.
