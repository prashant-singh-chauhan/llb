Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Article 21)

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 3.2 D

No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

Article 21 of the Constitution secures two rights to every person which are stated as following:

  • Right to life : means something more than mere animal existence. It means and includes all those means and ways by which life can be enjoyed.
  • Right to personal liberty : means the liberty of an individual to behave as one pleases except for those restraints imposed by law.
1. Right to life does not mean mere animal existence but it means the right to live with human dignity.

The right to life does not mean merely the physical existence of a person. It means the right to live with human dignity and enjoy the basic necessities required for a meaningful life. The Supreme Court has interpreted Article 21 broadly to protect several rights necessary for a dignified life.

The right to life includes all those means and facilities through which a person can live with dignity. It includes basic necessities such as adequate food, clothing and shelter, along with the freedom to express oneself and maintain social relationships. Thus, Article 21 protects not merely survival but also the quality and dignity of human life.

Major Cases on the Right to Life with Human Dignity

1. Francis Coralie Mullin v. Union Territory of Delhi (1981)

Citation: AIR 1981 SC 746

Facts / Issue:

The case concerned the scope of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and whether the right to life meant only physical existence or included the facilities necessary for living with human dignity.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court held that the right to life includes the right to live with human dignity. It includes the bare necessities of life, such as adequate nutrition, clothing and shelter, along with facilities for reading, writing, expressing oneself, moving freely and maintaining social relationships.

Principle of the Judgment:

Right to life means more than mere animal existence. It includes the basic necessities and freedoms required for a dignified human life.

2. Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985)

Citation: AIR 1986 SC 180

Facts / Issue:

This case is popularly known as the Pavement Dwellers Case. It concerned the connection between the right to life and the means by which a person earns a livelihood.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court held that the right to life under Article 21 includes the right to livelihood.

Principle of the Judgment:

Livelihood is essential for survival. Therefore, the right to life cannot be separated from the means necessary for a person to maintain life.

3. Chameli Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1996)

Citation: (1996) 2 SCC 549

Facts / Issue:

The case concerned the recognition of the right to shelter as part of the right to life under Article 21.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court recognised the right to shelter as an important part of Article 21.

Principle of the Judgment:

The right to life includes the right to shelter because suitable shelter is necessary for living with human dignity.

4. Parmanand Katara v. Union of India (1989)

Citation: AIR 1989 SC 2039

Facts / Issue:

The case concerned the recognition of the right to health and medical assistance as part of the protection of life under Article 21.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court recognised the importance of providing medical assistance to protect human life.

Principle of the Judgment:

The protection of human life and access to medical assistance are important aspects of Article 21.

5. Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991)

Citation: AIR 1991 SC 420

Facts / Issue:

The case concerned the recognition of the right to pollution-free air and water as part of the right to life under Article 21.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court recognised the right to enjoy pollution-free water and air as part of the right to life.

Principle of the Judgment:

The right to a pollution-free environment is necessary for protecting human life and health.

6. Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka (1992)

Citation: AIR 1992 SC 1858

Facts / Issue:

The case concerned the recognition of the right to education in relation to Article 21 of the Constitution.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court recognised the importance of education as a right connected with the right to life.

Principle of the Judgment:

Education is important for the development of an individual and for living a life with dignity.

7. Ramlila Maidan Incident v. Home Secretary, Union of India (2012)

Citation: (2012) 5 SCC 3136

Facts / Issue:

The case concerned the recognition of the right to sleep peacefully as part of the right to life under Article 21.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court recognised that a citizen has a right to sound sleep because it is fundamental to life.

Principle of the Judgment:

Sleep is a fundamental and basic human requirement. It is necessary for maintaining health and survival. Therefore, the right to sleep peacefully forms part of the right to life under Article 21.

2. Expanding Horizons of Right to Life and Personal Liberty as guaranteed under Indian Constitution.

Introduction:

Article 21 of the Constitution of India provides that “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.” It guarantees two important rights, namely the right to life and the right to personal liberty. The Supreme Court has gradually expanded the meaning of Article 21 to include several rights necessary for living with human dignity.

1. Meaning and Scope of Article 21:

The right to life does not mean mere animal existence. It includes all the means and facilities necessary for enjoying life with dignity. The right to personal liberty means the freedom of an individual to behave as one pleases, subject to restrictions imposed by law.

In A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), the Supreme Court adopted a narrow interpretation of Article 21. However, in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Court held that the procedure established by law must be fair, just and reasonable and must not be arbitrary or oppressive. This judgment greatly expanded the protection available under Article 21.

2. Expansion of the Right to Life through Decided Cases:

(a) Right to Live with Human Dignity:

In Francis Coralie Mullin v. Union Territory of Delhi (1981), the Supreme Court held that the right to life includes the right to live with human dignity. It includes basic necessities such as adequate nutrition, clothing and shelter, along with opportunities for education, expression and social interaction.

(b) Right to Livelihood:

In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1986), popularly known as the Pavement Dwellers Case, the Supreme Court held that the right to livelihood is an important part of the right to life because livelihood is essential for survival.

(c) Right to Privacy:

In Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), a nine-judge Bench unanimously declared that the right to privacy is a fundamental right. The Court recognised privacy as an essential aspect of personal liberty and human dignity. Similarly, in R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu (1994), the Court recognised the right to privacy, also known as the right to be let alone.

(d) Right to Shelter and Health:

In Chameli Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1996), the Supreme Court recognised the right to shelter as part of Article 21. In Parmanand Katara v. Union of India (1989), the Court emphasised that immediate medical assistance must be provided to protect human life.

(e) Right to Sleep:

In Ramlila Maidan Incident v. Home Secretary, Union of India (2012), the Supreme Court recognised that peaceful sleep is a basic human requirement and forms part of the right to life.

(f) Right to Die with Dignity:

In Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab (1996), the Court held that Article 21 does not include a general right to die. However, in Common Cause v. Union of India (2018), the Court recognised the right to die with dignity in specified circumstances involving terminally ill patients and permitted advance directives for medical treatment.

3. Other Rights under Article 21:

The Supreme Court has also recognised several other rights within the scope of Article 21, including the right to free legal aid in M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra, the right to speedy trial in Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar, the right to pollution-free air and water in Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, and the right to marry a person of one's choice in Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K.M.

Conclusion:

The Supreme Court has progressively widened the scope of Article 21 through judicial interpretation. It now protects not merely physical existence but also human dignity, privacy, livelihood, health, shelter and personal freedom. Thus, Article 21 is a living constitutional guarantee that protects the conditions necessary for a meaningful and dignified life.

3. Environment protection structure is the result of extended interpretation of Article 21.

Introduction:

Article 21 of the Constitution of India provides that “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.” The right to life does not mean mere physical existence; it includes the right to live with human dignity. Through its broad interpretation of Article 21, the Supreme Court has recognised that a clean and healthy environment is necessary for protecting human life. Therefore, environmental protection has become an important part of the expanded meaning of Article 21.

Meaning of Environmental Protection under Article 21:

Environmental protection means safeguarding the natural surroundings necessary for human health and survival. Clean air and pure water are basic necessities of life. Air and water pollution can seriously affect human health and make dignified living difficult.

Although Article 21 expressly mentions the right to life and personal liberty, the Supreme Court has interpreted the right to life broadly to include the right to pollution-free air and water. This interpretation establishes a connection between environmental protection and the fundamental right to life.

Most Important Judicial Cases

1. Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991)

Facts :

Subhash Kumar filed a petition alleging that West Bokaro Collieries and Tata Iron and Steel Company were discharging industrial waste in the form of slurry into the Bokaro River. He claimed that the pollution was making the river water unsuitable for drinking and irrigation and was harming public health. He approached the Supreme Court seeking directions to prevent the alleged pollution.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court dismissed the petition because it found that the petitioner's purpose was personal rather than genuine public interest. However, the Court recognised the importance of protecting the environment and held that the right to life under Article 21 includes the right to enjoy pollution-free water and air.

Principle of the Judgment:

The right to life includes the right to enjoy pollution-free air and water for the full enjoyment of life. A citizen may approach the Court to prevent pollution through a genuine public-interest petition. This case established an important constitutional basis for environmental protection under Article 21.

2.Murli S. Deora v. Union of India (2002)

Citation: AIR 2002 SC 40

Facts :

The case concerned the harmful effects of smoking in public places and the health risks faced by non-smokers who were exposed to tobacco smoke.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court recognised the need to prohibit smoking in public places to protect people from the harmful effects of passive smoking.

Principle of the Judgment:

The right to life under Article 21 includes protection against air pollution caused by smoking in public places. Non-smokers have a right not to be exposed to tobacco smoke against their wishes. This case demonstrates that the right to life includes the protection of public health and the enjoyment of a healthy environment.

Importance of the Extended Interpretation of Article 21:

1. Protection of human health: A clean environment is necessary for maintaining health and preventing harm caused by polluted air and water.

2. Protection of human dignity: The right to life includes the conditions necessary for living with dignity. Environmental pollution can interfere with these conditions.

3. Responsibility of the State: The constitutional protection of life supports the need for appropriate measures to prevent environmental pollution and safeguard public health.

4. Role of the judiciary: The Supreme Court has expanded the meaning of Article 21 to protect rights that are essential for human survival and well-being. Environmental protection is an important example of this judicial interpretation.

5. Public interest: Genuine public-interest proceedings can help citizens seek judicial protection against environmental pollution.

Conclusion:

The extended interpretation of Article 21 has made environmental protection an important aspect of the fundamental right to life. The decision in Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991) recognised that pollution-free air and water are necessary for enjoying life fully. Thus, Article 21 protects not merely physical existence but also the environmental conditions required for healthy, dignified and meaningful human life.

4. "Criminal justice system is the result of interpretation of Article 21 of the Constitution of India." Discuss with the help of decided cases.

Introduction:

Article 21 of the Constitution of India provides that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. Through judicial interpretation, the Supreme Court has developed important safeguards relating to arrest, detention, legal aid, speedy trial and the treatment of prisoners. These safeguards have strengthened the Indian criminal justice system.

1. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

Facts : The Government impounded Maneka Gandhi's passport in the interest of the general public and initially refused to disclose the reasons. She challenged the order before the Supreme Court, arguing that it violated her fundamental rights.

Judgment and Principle: The Court held that the procedure established by law must be fair, just and reasonable. This principle requires fairness in State action affecting personal liberty and provides the constitutional foundation for fair criminal proceedings.

2. Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979)

Facts : A large number of undertrial prisoners in Bihar had remained in jail for years without their trials beginning. Some had already spent more time in prison than the maximum punishment they could receive if convicted.

Judgment and Principle: The Supreme Court ordered the release of certain prisoners and recognised the right to a speedy trial under Article 21. Unreasonable delay in criminal proceedings violates personal liberty and the requirements of fair justice.

3. M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra (1978)

Facts : M.H. Hoskot was convicted by a Sessions Court, and the High Court later enhanced his sentence to three years' imprisonment. There was a serious delay in obtaining the High Court judgment, which affected his opportunity to pursue a further appeal.

Judgment and Principle: The Supreme Court emphasised the importance of providing a free copy of the judgment and free legal assistance to eligible prisoners who cannot afford a lawyer. Effective access to an appeal is an important safeguard of fair procedure under Article 21.

4. Prem Shankar Shukla v. Delhi Administration (1980)

Facts : Prem Shankar Shukla, an undertrial prisoner in Tihar Jail, sent a telegram to a Supreme Court judge complaining that he and other prisoners were routinely handcuffed while being taken to court.

Judgment and Principle: The Supreme Court held that routine and unjustified handcuffing violates fundamental rights. Restraints must not be imposed arbitrarily, and prisoners must be treated with human dignity.

5. Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration (1980)

Facts : The case concerned the treatment of prisoners and protection against unlawful solitary confinement and harsh prison conditions. The Court examined whether imprisonment permitted authorities to impose restrictions beyond those authorised by law.

Judgment and Principle: The Supreme Court recognised protection against unlawful solitary confinement. Prisoners do not lose all fundamental rights upon imprisonment, and their dignity must be respected.

6. D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997)

Facts : D.K. Basu, Executive Chairman of Legal Aid Services, West Bengal, wrote to the Chief Justice of India about newspaper reports concerning deaths in police custody. Another petition concerning a custodial death was considered along with his case.

Judgment and Principle: The Supreme Court issued important guidelines for arrest and detention, including preparation of an arrest memo, informing relatives, maintaining official records and providing medical examinations. These safeguards aim to prevent custodial torture, illegal detention and deaths in custody.

Critical Analysis:

These judgments demonstrate that Article 21 protects accused persons and prisoners against arbitrary State action. The rights to speedy trial and legal aid promote fair proceedings, while safeguards against handcuffing, solitary confinement and custodial violence protect human dignity. However, effective implementation is essential to ensure that these constitutional protections work in practice.

Conclusion:

The Supreme Court has strengthened the Indian criminal justice system through its interpretation of Article 21. Fair procedure, speedy trial, legal aid and humane treatment are essential safeguards of personal liberty. Criminal justice must punish offenders according to law while protecting the fundamental rights and dignity of every person.

5. "No person shall be deprived of his life except according to the procedure established by law." — Article 21. Discuss.

Introduction:

Article 21 of the Constitution of India provides: “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.” It protects every person against arbitrary deprivation of life and personal liberty. The Supreme Court has gradually expanded the meaning of this provision to ensure that any restriction on personal liberty follows a fair, just and reasonable procedure.

Meaning of Procedure Established by Law:

It means that the State can deprive a person of life or personal liberty only by following a procedure prescribed by law. However, the procedure must also satisfy the requirements of fairness and justice, as explained by the Supreme Court.

1. A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950)

Facts :

A.K. Gopalan was a Communist leader who had been detained by the authorities in Madras. In 1950, he was detained under the Preventive Detention Act, 1950. He challenged his detention before the Supreme Court, arguing that the Act violated his fundamental rights, including those protected by Articles 19 and 21.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court adopted a narrow interpretation of Article 21. It held that “procedure established by law” meant a procedure prescribed by a law enacted by the Legislature. The Court largely upheld the Act, while declaring Section 14 invalid.

Principle of the Judgment:

A law duly enacted by the Legislature could deprive a person of life or personal liberty without the additional requirement that its procedure be fair or reasonable.

2. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

Facts :

Maneka Gandhi's passport was impounded by the Government on 2 July 1977 in the interest of the general public. When she requested the reasons, the Government refused to disclose them. She challenged the order before the Supreme Court, arguing that it violated her fundamental rights, including her right to personal liberty.

Judgment of the Court:

The Supreme Court held that the procedure prescribed by law must be fair, just and reasonable and must not be arbitrary, oppressive or fanciful. The Court rejected the narrow approach adopted in A.K. Gopalan.

Principle of the Judgment:

Any procedure affecting personal liberty must satisfy the requirements of fairness and natural justice. This judgment greatly expanded the protection provided by Article 21.

3. Francis Coralie Mullin v. Union Territory of Delhi (1981)

Facts :

Francis Coralie Mullin was detained under the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act. Restrictions were imposed on her interviews with family members and legal counsel. She challenged these restrictions as violations of her fundamental rights.

Judgment and Principle:

The Supreme Court held that the right to life includes the right to live with human dignity. It includes basic necessities such as nutrition, clothing and shelter, along with opportunities for expression and social interaction.

4. Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1986)

Facts :

Pavement dwellers and slum residents in Bombay challenged the proposed removal of their dwellings. They argued that eviction would deprive them of their livelihood because they lived near their workplaces and could not afford alternative accommodation.

Judgment and Principle:

The Supreme Court held that the right to livelihood forms part of the right to life. However, there is no absolute right to occupy public pavements. State action affecting livelihood must follow the procedure established by law.

Conclusion:

Article 21 has developed from a narrow protection against deprivation of life and personal liberty into a broad constitutional guarantee. The decisions in A.K. Gopalan, Maneka Gandhi, Francis Coralie Mullin and Olga Tellis demonstrate this development. The Supreme Court has established that life means more than physical survival and personal liberty cannot be restricted through arbitrary procedures. Article 21 therefore protects human dignity and requires fairness whenever the State interferes with life or personal liberty.

6. The right to life and personal liberty has undergone many changes and innovations from Gopalan to the recent case law. Critically evaluate the innovations.

Introduction:

Article 21 of the Constitution of India provides that “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.” The Supreme Court has interpreted this Article broadly over time. From the narrow interpretation in A.K. Gopalan to the recognition of privacy and dignity in recent cases, Article 21 has undergone significant changes and innovations.

1. A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950)

Facts / Issue: The case raised the question of whether a law enacted by the Legislature could deprive a person of personal liberty without the Court examining whether the procedure was fair or reasonable.

Judgment: The Supreme Court adopted a narrow interpretation of Article 21. It held that “procedure established by law” meant a procedure prescribed by a law duly enacted by the Legislature.

Principle: The Court did not require the procedure to be fair, just or reasonable merely because it affected personal liberty.

2. Kharak Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1963)

Facts: Kharak Singh challenged certain police regulations, arguing that they violated his freedom of movement and personal liberty.

Judgment: The majority explained that personal liberty was a broad expression covering different rights. However, it did not recognise privacy as a guaranteed constitutional right. The minority considered privacy an essential part of personal liberty.

Principle: The case clarified the broad scope of personal liberty and prepared the ground for its further development.

3. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

Facts: Maneka Gandhi challenged action affecting her passport and argued that her personal liberty had been violated.

Judgment: The Supreme Court rejected the narrow approach of A.K. Gopalan and held that the procedure established by law must be fair, just and reasonable, not arbitrary or oppressive.

Principle: This judgment was a major constitutional innovation. It introduced a fairness requirement into Article 21 and greatly strengthened protection against arbitrary State action.

4. Francis Coralie Mullin v. Union Territory of Delhi (1981)

Facts: The case concerned restrictions on the interviews of a detained person with family members and legal counsel.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that the right to life includes the right to live with human dignity, including basic necessities such as nutrition, clothing and shelter.

Principle: Life means more than mere physical existence. Article 21 protects the conditions necessary for dignified living.

5. Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)

Facts: The case arose from a challenge to the Aadhaar scheme and raised the question of whether privacy was protected as a fundamental right.

Judgment: A unanimous nine-judge Bench of the Supreme Court declared that the right to privacy is a fundamental right and rejected earlier decisions denying constitutional protection to privacy.

Principle: Privacy forms an essential part of personal liberty, individual dignity and the protection of personal life.

Critical Evaluation:

These decisions show a major development in constitutional law. Article 21 now covers dignity, privacy, livelihood, shelter, health, speedy trial and other essential rights. This wider interpretation makes the Constitution responsive to changing human needs. However, courts must ensure that such expansion remains consistent with constitutional principles and that restrictions on liberty follow fair procedures.

Conclusion:

The journey from A.K. Gopalan to K.S. Puttaswamy demonstrates the transformation of Article 21 from a narrow procedural guarantee into a broad protection of human dignity and freedom. The Supreme Court has established that the right to life means more than mere existence, and personal liberty must be protected through fair, just and reasonable procedures.
