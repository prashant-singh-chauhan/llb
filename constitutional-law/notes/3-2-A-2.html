Module 03 · Fundamental Rights

Prohibition of Discrimination on Grounds of Religion, Race, Caste, Sex or Place of Birth

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 3.2 A2 - Article 15

1. Introduction

Article 15 of the Constitution of India prohibits the State from discriminating against citizens on certain specified grounds. It promotes equality and protects citizens against unfair treatment.

Grounds of Discrimination

  • Religion
  • Race
  • Caste
  • Sex
  • Place of birth

Article 15(1): The State shall not discriminate against any citizen only on the grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth, or any of them.

Article 15(2): provides that no citizen shall, on the grounds mentioned above, be subject to any disability, liability, restriction or condition with regard to:

(a) Access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment; or

(b) The use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained wholly or partly out of State funds or dedicated to the use of the general public.

Article 15(3): provides that nothing in Article 15 shall prevent the State from making any special provision for women and children.

Article 15(4): provides that nothing in Article 15 or Article 29(2) shall prevent the State from making any special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes.

Article 15(5) provides that nothing in Article 15 or Article 19(1)(g) shall prevent the State from making any special provision, by law, for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, in relation to their admission to educational institutions, including private educational institutions, whether aided or unaided by the State, except minority educational institutions referred to in Article 30(1). It was inserted by the 93rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2005, to enable special provisions for educational advancement.

Article 15(6) provides that nothing in Article 15 or Article 19(1)(g) shall prevent the State from making any special provision for the advancement of any economically weaker sections (EWS) of citizens, other than the classes mentioned in clauses (4) and (5) of Article 15. Such provisions may include reservation in educational institutions, including private educational institutions, whether aided or unaided by the State, except minority educational institutions referred to in Article 30(1). The reservation shall be in addition to the existing reservations and shall be subject to a maximum of 10% of the total seats in each category.

Reference: Article 29(2) provides that no citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them.

Reference: Article 19(1)(g) provides that all citizens shall have the right to practise any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade or business.

Reference: Article 30(1) provides that all minorities, whether based on religion or language, shall have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

2. Who are Socially and Educationally Backward Classes?

Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs) are groups of citizens who are considered backward in society and education and therefore need special measures for their advancement. Their backwardness is determined through relevant social and educational indicators and, where applicable, constitutional and statutory mechanisms. The Constitution permits the State to make special provisions for their educational and social advancement. Articles 15(4), 15(5) and 16(4) provide important constitutional safeguards for such classes.

3. What is the meaning of the word “only” as used under Article 15(1) of the Constitution?

Introduction: Article 15(1) of the Indian Constitution prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen only on the grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

Meaning of “Only”: The word “only” means that discrimination is prohibited when it is based solely on one or more of the grounds mentioned in Article 15(1). If discrimination is based on other valid grounds and not merely on these prohibited grounds, Article 15(1) may not apply.

Legal Importance: The word “only” does not mean that the State can freely discriminate whenever another ground is mentioned. The real question is whether a prohibited ground is the sole basis of the discrimination. If a prohibited ground is combined with another ground, the case must be examined carefully to determine whether the action is discriminatory under the Constitution.

Example: If a government institution refuses admission to a student solely because of the student's caste, it violates Article 15(1). The State cannot justify such discrimination by treating caste as a valid reason.

Conclusion: The word “only” limits the specific prohibition under Article 15(1) to discrimination based solely on the listed grounds. Its purpose is to protect citizens from discrimination while allowing the State to make decisions on lawful and constitutionally valid grounds.

4. Explain the Provisions of Article 15(5) of the Constitution (2015)

Introduction: Article 15(5) of the Indian Constitution empowers the State to make special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes of citizens, Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs).

Main Provisions:

1. Special Provisions: The State may make laws to provide special facilities for the educational advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs.

2. Admission to Educational Institutions: These provisions may relate to admission to educational institutions, including government and private educational institutions.

3. Aided and Unaided Institutions: The provision applies to private educational institutions whether they receive government aid or not.

4. Exception for Minority Institutions: Article 15(5) does not apply to minority educational institutions protected under Article 30(1) of the Constitution.

5. Constitutional Amendment: Article 15(5) was inserted by the 93rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2005, to enable special provisions for educational advancement.

Conclusion: Article 15(5) promotes educational equality and social justice by allowing special provisions for disadvantaged groups. It aims to improve their access to education while protecting the constitutional rights of minority educational institutions.

5. Explain the Provisions of Article 15(6) of the Constitution (2015)

Introduction:

Article 15 of the Indian Constitution prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Article 15(6) was inserted by the 103rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019. It provides special provisions for the advancement of Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) of society.

1. Special Provisions for Economically Weaker Sections:

Article 15(6)(a) empowers the State to make special provisions for the advancement of economically weaker sections, excluding the classes covered under Articles 15(4) and 15(5).

2. Reservation in Educational Institutions:

Article 15(6)(b) allows the State to provide reservation for EWS in educational institutions, including private educational institutions, whether aided or unaided by the State. However, this provision does not apply to minority educational institutions covered under Article 30(1).

3. Maximum Limit of Reservation:

The provision permits up to 10% additional reservation for economically weaker sections in educational institutions. This reservation is in addition to the existing reservation.

4. Identification of Economically Weaker Sections:

The State is authorised to determine the criteria for identifying economically weaker sections. These criteria may include family income and other indicators of economic disadvantage.

5. Objective of Article 15(6):

The main objective is to provide educational opportunities to economically weaker persons who are not covered by the existing reservation provisions for backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

Conclusion:

Article 15(6) is an important constitutional provision that promotes educational opportunities for economically weaker sections. It permits up to 10% additional reservation in educational institutions, subject to constitutional conditions, while excluding minority educational institutions protected under Article 30(1).
6. Critically examine the reservation policy as guaranteed under Indian Constitution. Refer to decided cases.

Introduction: Reservation is a system through which the government provides special opportunities in education, public employment and political representation to disadvantaged sections of society. Its main purpose is to promote equality, social justice and adequate representation. The Indian Constitution permits reservation to reduce the effects of historical discrimination and social inequality.

Constitutional Provisions:

1. Article 15(4): It allows the State to make special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs).

2. Article 15(5): It permits special provisions relating to admission to educational institutions, including private aided and unaided institutions, except minority educational institutions protected under Article 30(1).

3. Article 16(4): It permits reservation in public employment for backward classes that, in the opinion of the State, are not adequately represented in government services.

4. Article 16(4A): It permits reservation in promotion for SCs and STs, subject to constitutional conditions.

5. Articles 330 and 332: They provide for reservation of seats for SCs and STs in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, respectively.

6. Articles 15(6) and 16(6): They permit up to 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS), subject to constitutional conditions.

Important Judicial Decisions:

1. State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)

Facts: The Madras Government had introduced a communal Government Order that allocated seats in educational institutions among different communities on the basis of religion, caste and other community categories. The arrangement was challenged as violating fundamental rights.

Judgment: The Supreme Court struck down the communal allocation because it violated Article 29(2) of the Constitution.

Principle of Judgment: Fundamental Rights prevail over conflicting Directive Principles of State Policy. The judgment led to the First Constitutional Amendment, 1951, which inserted Article 15(4) to permit special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

2. Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992)

Facts: The Central Government decided to implement the Mandal Commission's recommendation to provide 27% reservation in central government employment for Other Backward Classes (OBCs). The decision was challenged before the Supreme Court.

Judgment: The Supreme Court upheld 27% reservation for OBCs, subject to the exclusion of the creamy layer. It generally fixed a 50% ceiling on reservations, subject to exceptional circumstances, and held that Article 16(4) did not permit reservation in promotions.

Principle of Judgment: Reservation is a means of achieving substantive equality. The creamy layer among OBCs must be excluded, and reservation must ordinarily remain within the 50% ceiling. The ruling concerning promotion led to the constitutional amendment introducing Article 16(4A).

3. M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006)

Facts: The constitutional validity of amendments permitting reservation in promotion for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, including consequential seniority, was challenged before the Supreme Court.

Judgment: The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional amendments, while laying down conditions that States had to satisfy when providing reservation in promotion.

Principle of Judgment: Reservation in promotion is constitutionally permissible, but it is not automatic. The State must consider relevant factors, including inadequate representation and administrative efficiency under Article 335. The original judgment also required consideration of backwardness and overall administrative efficiency. Later, the Supreme Court clarified aspects of these requirements in subsequent cases.

4. Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022)

Facts: The 103rd Constitutional Amendment introduced Articles 15(6) and 16(6), allowing up to 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS). The amendment was challenged on constitutional grounds.

Judgment: By a 3:2 majority, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the 103rd Constitutional Amendment.

Principle of Judgment: Economic disadvantage can be a basis for special provisions and reservation. The exclusion of SCs, STs and OBCs already covered by existing reservation provisions from the EWS category was held constitutionally valid by the majority. The Court also upheld the additional 10% reservation beyond the existing reservation limits.

Critical Examination:

1. Promotes Social Justice: Reservation provides opportunities to communities that have faced historical discrimination and social exclusion. It helps improve their access to education, employment and public institutions.

2. Ensures Representation: It helps disadvantaged groups obtain representation in government services and legislatures, making public institutions more inclusive.

3. Risk of Unequal Distribution: Sometimes, the benefits of reservation may reach relatively better-off members of a backward class, while the most disadvantaged members receive fewer benefits. Effective implementation and suitable review are therefore important.

4. Need for Constitutional Limits: Reservation must follow constitutional requirements and judicial guidelines. It should not be treated as an unlimited right to reservation, and equality of opportunity must remain an important consideration.

5. State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024)

Facts: The Punjab Government introduced a provision giving preferential treatment to Balmikis and Mazhabi Sikhs within the reservation quota for Scheduled Castes (SCs) in public employment. The provision was challenged, and the Punjab and Haryana High Court struck it down, relying on the earlier judgment in E. V. Chinnaiah v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2004), which had held that sub-classification within Scheduled Castes was not permissible.

Judgment: On 1 August 2024, a seven-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court, by a majority of 6:1, held that sub-classification within Scheduled Castes for reservation is constitutionally permissible. It overruled the earlier decision in E. V. Chinnaiah

Principle of Judgment: The State may divide Scheduled Castes into sub-groups to ensure that the benefits of reservation reach the more disadvantaged groups. However, such classification must be supported by reliable empirical data showing differences in backwardness and inadequate representation. The State cannot make such classifications arbitrarily or merely for political reasons. The decision must also satisfy the requirements of equality under Article 14.

E. V. Chinnaiah v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2004)

Facts: The Andhra Pradesh Government enacted the Scheduled Castes (Rationalisation of Reservations) Act, 2000. It divided the Scheduled Castes listed for the State into four groups and allocated separate portions of the existing reservation quota to each group. The purpose was to ensure that relatively disadvantaged groups within the Scheduled Castes also received reservation benefits. The law was challenged before the Supreme Court.

Judgment: In 2004, a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court struck down the law. It held that the State could not sub-classify the Scheduled Castes for distributing reservation benefits in this manner. The Court considered the Scheduled Castes in the Presidential List to be one single class for the purpose of the reservation framework.

Principle of Judgment: The Court held that a State could not alter or divide the Presidential List of Scheduled Castes notified under Article 341. It considered such sub-classification unconstitutional and beyond the State's legislative power.

Overruling in State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024): On 1 August 2024, a seven-judge Constitution Bench, by a 6:1 majority, overruled the decision in E. V. Chinnaiah. The Supreme Court held that States may sub-classify Scheduled Castes for reservation benefits if the classification is supported by relevant empirical data and satisfies constitutional requirements. The Presidential List itself cannot be altered by the State; sub-classification concerns the distribution of reservation benefits, not the removal or addition of castes to that List.

Conclusion: Reservation is an important constitutional tool for achieving social justice and substantive equality. However, it must be implemented fairly, based on relevant evidence and within constitutional limits. Regular review and effective policies are necessary to ensure that its benefits reach those who need them most.

7. Reservation Policy under the Indian Constitution is Protective Discrimination in Favour of Weaker Sections. Do You Agree? Discuss with the Help of Decided Cases.

Introduction: Yes, reservation policy under the Indian Constitution is a form of protective discrimination in favour of weaker sections of society. Protective discrimination means making special provisions for disadvantaged groups to overcome historical injustice and achieve equality. The Constitution allows such provisions to promote social justice and equal opportunities.

Constitutional Provisions:

1. Article 15(4): It permits special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs).

2. Article 15(5): It permits special provisions for admission to educational institutions, including private aided and unaided institutions, except minority educational institutions protected under Article 30(1).

3. Article 16(4): It permits reservation in public employment for backward classes that are not adequately represented in State services.

4. Article 16(4A): It permits reservation in promotion for SCs and STs, subject to constitutional conditions.

5. Articles 15(6) and 16(6): They permit special provisions, including up to 10% reservation, for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS), subject to constitutional conditions.

Importance of Protective Discrimination:

1. Social Justice: Reservation helps address historical caste discrimination, social exclusion and inequality.

2. Equality of Opportunity: It improves access to education and public employment for disadvantaged groups.

3. Adequate Representation: It helps groups that have been underrepresented participate in government services and public institutions.

4. Inclusive Development: It promotes the participation of disadvantaged communities in national development.
