Right to Equality (Articles 14 to 18)

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 3.2 A - Article 14

1. Article 14

Article 14 of the Indian Constitution provides equality before law and equal protection of laws. It ensures that every person receives equal treatment under the law.

2. Equality Before Law

Equality before law that means that no person is above the law. Every person, whether rich or poor, an ordinary citizen or a government official, is subject to the law. Nobody can claim special privileges simply because of their position or wealth. It is a negative concept because it prevents special privileges.

3. Equal Protection of Laws

Equal protection of laws that means that people in similar circumstances should be treated equally. The government must provide equal legal protection to everyone. However, different treatment is permitted when there is a reasonable basis for the difference. It is a positive concept because it requires the State to ensure equal treatment.

4. Main Difference between Equality before law and Equal protection of laws

Equality before law prohibits special privileges, whereas equal protection of laws requires equal treatment in similar situations. The former emphasises equality in the eyes of the law, while the latter emphasises equality in the application of the law.

5. Illustrations - Equality before law.

1. No Special Privileges: If a minister commits a crime, the same criminal law generally applies as it does to an ordinary citizen. No one is above the law.

2. Reasonable Classification: Equality does not mean everyone must be treated identically in every situation. The government may make special provisions for women, children and disadvantaged groups. Reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in education or public employment aims to promote equality.

3. Taxation: People with higher incomes may pay more tax than people with lower incomes. This may be valid because taxation can be based on the ability to pay.

4. Special Courts: Special courts may deal with particular offences, such as corruption, where there is a reasonable basis for the distinction. Such arrangements must not be arbitrary.

5. Foreigners: Article 14 protects citizens and foreigners because it applies to every person. However, enemy aliens may be treated differently in certain circumstances, including matters of national security.

Exceptions and Conclusion

The right to equality is not absolute. Reasonable classification is permitted when it has a clear basis and a rational connection with the purpose of the law. Article 361 protects the President and Governors from being answerable to courts for the exercise of their official powers and duties. Certain legal proceedings against them are restricted during their term, but this protection does not place all government actions beyond judicial review.

Conclusion: Article 14 balances equal treatment with reasonable differences. It safeguards fairness, dignity and justice and plays an important role in strengthening Indian democracy.

6. Doctrine of Reasonable Classification

The Doctrine of Reasonable Classification is a principle of the Indian Constitution that allows the government to treat different groups of people differently when there is a reasonable basis for doing so. It is based on Article 14 of the Constitution, which provides for equality before the law and equal protection of the laws.

This doctrine means that equality does not require everyone to be treated in exactly the same way in every situation. People who are in different circumstances may be treated differently to achieve fairness and justice. However, the government cannot make distinctions based on personal wishes, favouritism or arbitrary reasons.

For example, the government may provide special benefits to economically weaker sections of society to improve their living conditions. Such classification may be valid if it is based on reasonable grounds and serves a proper purpose.

The Supreme Court has laid down two essential conditions for reasonable classification:

  1. Intelligible Differentia: There must be a clear and understandable difference between the people included in a group and those excluded from it.
  2. Rational Nexus: The difference must have a reasonable connection with the object that the law seeks to achieve.

Both conditions must be satisfied for a classification to be valid.

Thus, the Doctrine of Reasonable Classification allows necessary distinctions while preventing unfair discrimination. Its main purpose is to ensure that laws are reasonable, fair and consistent with the principle of equality under Article 14.

Evolution through Judicial Interpretation: The Doctrine of Reasonable Classification has been developed by the Supreme Court through various judgments while interpreting Article 14 of the Indian Constitution.

1. State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952)

Facts: The West Bengal Special Courts Act, 1950, empowered the State Government to send certain cases to special courts for speedy trials. The government had wide discretion to select cases for these courts without clear guidelines.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that the provision allowing arbitrary selection of cases violated Article 14 of the Constitution because it did not provide a reasonable basis for classification.

Principle: Classification must be reasonable and must not result in arbitrary discrimination.

2. Budhan Choudhry v. State of Bihar (1955)

Facts: The appellants were accused of committing criminal offences and challenged their trial before a magistrate empowered under Section 30 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898. They argued that the provision violated Article 14 because it allowed different procedures for different accused persons.

Judgment: The Supreme Court upheld the validity of the provision and explained that Article 14 prohibits class legislation but permits reasonable classification.

Principle: The Court clearly laid down the two essential conditions of reasonable classification:

  1. Intelligible Differentia.
  2. Rational Nexus.

These conditions ensure that classification is reasonable and has a proper connection with the objective of the law.

7. Class Legislation

Meaning of Class Legislation: Class legislation means making laws that give special privileges to a particular group of people or treat other groups unfairly without any reasonable basis. Such laws create unnecessary differences between people and violate the principle of equality.

Prohibition under Article 14: Article 14 does not allow the government to make arbitrary laws that favour one group over another without a valid reason. Every person must receive equal treatment in similar circumstances. However, the Constitution permits reasonable classification when there is a clear difference between groups and that difference has a reasonable connection with the purpose of the law.

8. Doctrine of Non-Arbitrariness

Doctorine of Non-Arbitrariness: means that the government must act fairly, reasonably and according to law. It cannot take decisions based on personal wishes, favouritism or without any valid reason. Every government action must follow proper rules and principles. The government must treat people equally in similar circumstances and must not discriminate unfairly. Article 14 of the Indian Constitution prohibits arbitrary actions by the State because they violate the right to equality.

Evolution through Judicial Interpretation: The Doctrine of Non-Arbitrariness has been developed by the Supreme Court through various judgments while interpreting Article 14 of the Indian Constitution.

1. E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974)

Facts: E.P. Royappa was a senior IAS officer who challenged his transfer from the post of Chief Secretary to another post. He argued that the transfer was arbitrary and violated his right to equality under Article 14.

Judgment: The Supreme Court did not find sufficient grounds to establish that the transfer was arbitrary. However, it laid down the important principle that equality and arbitrariness are opposites.

Principle: Any arbitrary action of the State violates Article 14 because it denies equality.

2. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

Facts: The government impounded Maneka Gandhi's passport under the Passports Act, 1967, without initially providing her an opportunity to be heard. She challenged the action as a violation of her fundamental rights.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that any procedure affecting personal liberty must be fair, just and reasonable. It also explained that Article 14 prohibits arbitrary State action and ensures fairness and equality of treatment.

Principle: Government actions and legal procedures must be fair and reasonable, not arbitrary or oppressive.

3. Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib Sehravardi (1981)

Facts: The case concerned admission to a Regional Engineering College in Srinagar. The admission process was challenged because excessive importance was given to marks awarded in the oral interview.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that excessive weightage given to the oral interview was arbitrary in the circumstances of the case and violated Article 14. The Court also explained that the principle of equality applies to State authorities and bodies that qualify as the State under Article 12.

Principle: Article 14 prohibits arbitrary State action and requires fairness and equality in decisions.

Conclusion

These three cases established that Article 14 is not limited to preventing discrimination. It also prohibits arbitrary actions by the government and requires fairness, reasonableness and equality in the administration of law.

9. "Equality is a dynamic concept with many aspects and dimensions and it can not be imprisoned within traditional limits."

Article 14: Dynamic Concept of Equality

Introduction

Article 14 of the Constitution of India is an important part of the country's democratic system. It guarantees the Right to Equality before the law and equal protection of the laws to all persons within the territory of India. Traditionally, equality was understood in a simple way: persons who are equal should be treated equally, while persons who are different may be treated differently through the test of "reasonable classification".

However, with changes in social, political, and economic conditions, the judiciary realized that strict rules were not enough to protect human rights. This led to the famous statement of the Supreme Court:

"Equality is a dynamic concept with many aspects and dimensions and it cannot be imprisoned within traditional limits."

Today, equality is a living principle that continues to develop to remove arbitrariness and injustice in all their forms.

The Traditional Approach: Reasonable Classification

To understand the development of equality, we must first study its traditional meaning. Initially, courts examined the validity of a law under Article 14 by applying the two tests of Reasonable Classification:

1. Intelligible Differentia: There must be a clear and understandable difference between the persons or things included in a group and those kept outside it.

2. Rational Nexus: There must be a reasonable connection between the basis of classification and the aim or purpose of the law.

Although this test prevents arbitrary class legislation, the Supreme Court soon realized that depending only on classification was too limited. Sometimes, State actions could be unfair, discriminatory, or unreasonable even when they appeared to satisfy the formal rules of classification.

Judicial Pronouncements Expanding the Dynamic Concept of Equality

The judiciary changed Article 14 from a simple rule into a strong protection against unfair State action through several important judgments:

1. E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974)

This case was a major turning point in Indian constitutional law. The Supreme Court moved away from the traditional limits of equality. Justice P.N. Bhagwati famously observed that equality is a dynamic concept with many aspects and dimensions, and it cannot be imprisoned within traditional limits.

Importantly, the Court established that equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies. If an action is arbitrary, it is automatically unequal. Therefore, any State action that is unreasonable, based on personal choice, or without proper justification violates Article 14, even if no formal classification is involved.

2. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

Building on the changing meaning of fundamental rights, the Supreme Court connected Article 14 with Articles 19 (freedom) and 21 (life and personal liberty). The Court held that any procedure that takes away a person's liberty must be "right, just, fair, and reasonable" and must not be arbitrary or oppressive. By including fairness within Article 14, the Court ensured that equality applies to both laws and the procedures followed by the State.

3. Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981)

Supporting the principle laid down in Royappa, the Supreme Court held that arbitrariness is completely against the rule of law. The Court stated that arbitrary action by the State directly violates Article 14. This widened the scope of equality to include administrative decisions, government service matters, and unfair actions by public institutions.

4. Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018)

In this important case, the Supreme Court applied the modern meaning of equality to strike down the part of an outdated law that criminalized consensual same-sex relations between adults. The Court emphasized that constitutional morality requires the State to respect individual freedom, dignity, and personal choices. This case showed that Article 14 protects marginalized groups against discrimination, social prejudice, and unfair treatment.

Dimensions of Modern Equality

Since equality is a changing concept, its scope now includes several modern aspects:

1. Substantive Equality: It goes beyond formal equality, which means treating everyone in the same way on paper. It recognizes that people who have faced disadvantages in the past may need special support, such as reservations and protective measures, to achieve real equality.

2. Anti-Stereotyping Principle: Modern judgments use Article 14 to challenge unfair beliefs about gender and society that limit individual freedom and dignity.

3. Non-Arbitrariness: Every legislative and executive action must be based on reason, fairness, and transparency and must not be arbitrary.

Conclusion

Article 14 is not a fixed rule that remains unchanged over time. Through progressive judicial decisions, the Supreme Court has expanded the meaning of equality beyond its traditional limits. By connecting equality with human dignity, fairness, and the absence of arbitrariness, the judiciary has ensured that the Right to Equality remains a strong and effective protection against injustice in a modern democratic society.

10. "Article 14 enunciates a vital principle which lies at the core of our republicanism shines like beacon light printing towards the goal of Classless-egalitarian-socio-economic order", Examine the above statement in the light of Indian case law.
Introduction

The statement that Article 14 is a beacon light pointing towards a classless and egalitarian socio-economic order explains the importance of equality in the Indian Constitution. Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws. Its main objective is to remove discrimination, reduce social and economic inequalities, and establish a just society where everyone enjoys equal rights and opportunities.

1. From Formal Equality to Substantive Equality

Formal equality means treating everyone equally before the law. However, people from weaker sections may face social and economic disadvantages. Therefore, the Constitution supports substantive equality, which means taking special measures to bring disadvantaged people closer to an equal position.

Case: State of Kerala v. N.M. Thomas (1976)

The Supreme Court recognized that equality does not always mean treating everyone in the same manner. Special provisions for disadvantaged groups can help achieve real equality and social justice.

2. Equality and Non-Arbitrariness

Article 14 prohibits arbitrary actions by the government. State authorities must act fairly, reasonably and according to the law. No person should suffer because of the unfair or unreasonable decisions of public authorities.

Case: E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974)

The Supreme Court held that equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies. Any arbitrary action by the State may violate Article 14. This judgment expanded the meaning of equality beyond the traditional concept of discrimination.

Case: Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib Sehravardi (1981)

The Court reaffirmed that arbitrariness is opposed to equality. Government decisions must follow constitutional principles and cannot be based on personal preferences or unfair considerations.

3. Protection of Weaker Sections

A classless society cannot be established while poor people and workers suffer from exploitation. Article 14, along with other constitutional provisions, supports the protection of weaker sections and promotes social and economic justice.

Case: Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984)

The Supreme Court addressed the condition of bonded labourers and emphasized the importance of protecting their human dignity. The judgment highlighted the responsibility of the State to prevent exploitation and enforce laws for the welfare of workers.

Case: Consumer Education and Research Centre v. Union of India (1995)

The Supreme Court emphasized the importance of workers' health, medical care and dignity. It recognized that protecting workers is essential for achieving social and economic justice.

4. Equality and Individual Dignity

Case: Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018)

The Supreme Court decriminalized consensual same-sex relations between adults by reading down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code. The judgment emphasized equality, individual liberty, dignity and protection against discrimination. It showed that equality must protect every individual from unjust treatment.

Conclusion

Article 14 is not merely a legal provision; it is an important instrument of social transformation. Through various judicial decisions, the Supreme Court has expanded equality to include fairness, non-arbitrariness, social justice and human dignity. Thus, Article 14 acts as a beacon light guiding India towards a classless and egalitarian socio-economic order where every person enjoys equal rights, status and opportunities.
