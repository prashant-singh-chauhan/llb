Module 01 · Nature of the Indian Constitution

Form of the Government

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 1.2

1. Introduction

Federalism is a system of government in which power is divided between the Central Government and State Governments according to the Constitution. Both governments have their own powers and responsibilities and work independently in their respective areas. Neither government has complete control over the other. This system helps prevent the concentration of all powers in one government and ensures that the country is governed properly.

In simple words, federalism means sharing power between the Central Government and State Governments, with each government responsible for different matters.

2. Meaning of Quasi-Federalism

Quasi-federalism means a system that combines the features of both federal and unitary governments. In India, powers are divided between the Centre and the States, but the Centre has been given stronger powers.

Constitutional scholar K.C. Wheare described the Indian Constitution as “almost quasi-federal,” with a strong unitary tendency. According to this view, the Indian system gives greater importance to the Centre than a classical federation does.

3. Federal Features of the Indian Constitution
1. Two Levels of Government:

There are two levels of government, generally the Central Government and State Governments. Both govern the same citizens but perform different functions.

2. Written Constitution:

A written Constitution clearly defines the powers, duties and responsibilities of each level of government.

3. Division of Powers:

The Constitution divides powers between the Central and State Governments. In India, these powers are divided through the Union List, State List and Concurrent List under the Seventh Schedule.

4. Supremacy of the Constitution:

The Constitution is the supreme law of the country. Both levels of government must work according to its provisions.

5. Rigidity of the Constitution:

The Constitution cannot be amended easily. Certain amendments affecting the federal system require approval by at least half of the State Legislatures.

6. Independent Judiciary:

The Supreme Court interprets the Constitution, settles disputes between the Centre and States, and protects the constitutional division of powers.

7. Financial Independence:

Both levels of government need sufficient financial resources to perform their duties. Tax-sharing arrangements help maintain financial balance.

8. Bicameral Legislature:

Many federal countries have two houses of Parliament. In India, the Rajya Sabha represents the States and Union Territories at the national level.

4. Unitary Features of the Indian Constitution
1. Strong Central Government

The Centre has wider powers than the States in several important matters. The Union List also contains subjects of national importance.

2. Single Citizenship

India provides for single citizenship. Every Indian is a citizen of India, and there is no separate state citizenship.

3. Appointment of Governors

Governors are appointed by the President. They serve as constitutional heads of the States and have certain constitutional responsibilities.

4. Emergency Provisions

Articles 352, 356 and 360 provide for national, state and financial emergencies respectively. These provisions can increase the powers of the Centre, subject to constitutional requirements.

5. Power to Change State Boundaries

Under Article 3, Parliament can form new States and alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing States. The views of the affected State Legislature must be sought, but its consent is not binding on Parliament.

6. Residuary Powers

Under Article 248 and Entry 97 of the Union List, Parliament has the power to legislate on subjects not mentioned in the State List or Concurrent List.

7. Integrated Judiciary and All India Services

India has an integrated judicial system, with the Supreme Court at the top and High Courts and subordinate courts below it. Services such as the IAS and IPS also help maintain administrative coordination between the Centre and the States.

5. Views of K.C. Wheare and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar

K.C. Wheare was a famous constitutional expert who described the Indian Constitution as “quasi-federal.” According to him, India has a federal system, but the Central Government is stronger than the State Governments.

His view is based on the following points:

  • The Constitution divides legislative powers between the Centre and States.
  • The Centre has greater powers in important matters.
  • The Centre can exercise special powers during emergencies.
  • Parliament can alter the boundaries and names of States under Article 3.

Therefore, Wheare believed that India does not follow a completely federal system like the United States.

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, the Chairman of the Drafting Committee, explained that the Indian Constitution was designed to work as a federal system in normal times but could become more unitary during emergencies.

He emphasised that the Constitution establishes a federal structure with a strong Centre to maintain national unity, security and stability. During emergencies, the Union can exercise additional powers according to constitutional provisions.

6. Judicial View: S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court recognised federalism as a basic feature of the Constitution. The judgment emphasised that States are not merely administrative units of the Centre and that their constitutional powers must be respected.

7. Critical Analysis

The Indian Constitution has a strong Centre, emergency provisions and a central role in changing State boundaries. These features support the view that India is quasi-federal.

However, the division of powers, elected State Governments, independent judiciary and special amendment procedure show that India is not a purely unitary state. The Centre cannot simply ignore the constitutional powers of the States.

8. Conclusion

The Indian Constitution is neither completely federal nor completely unitary. It combines federal principles with strong unitary features. Therefore, the most suitable description is that India is a quasi-federal state with a strong Centre. At the same time, federalism is a basic feature of the Constitution, and the constitutional powers of the States must be respected.

Parliamentary Form of Government
1. Introduction

The Parliamentary Form of Government is a system in which the executive is responsible to the legislature for its actions and policies. India has adopted this system from Britain. It is provided at the Union level under Articles 74 and 75 of the Indian Constitution and at the State level under Articles 163 and 164.

2. Main Features of Parliamentary Government

1. Two Executives: There is a nominal executive and a real executive. The President is the nominal head, while the Prime Minister is the real head of the Union Government.

2. Majority Party Rule: The political party or coalition that enjoys a majority in the Lok Sabha generally forms the government.

3. Collective Responsibility: Under Article 75(3), the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. If it loses the confidence of the House, it must resign.

4. Leadership of the Prime Minister: The Prime Minister leads the Council of Ministers and coordinates government policies.

5. Coordination Between Executive and Legislature: Ministers are generally members of Parliament and participate in legislative proceedings.

3. Conclusion

The parliamentary system promotes democratic accountability, cooperation and responsible government. However, it may also face problems such as political instability and party conflicts. Despite these limitations, it is suitable for India because it ensures that the government remains accountable to the elected representatives of the people.
