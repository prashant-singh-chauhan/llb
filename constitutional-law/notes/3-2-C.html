Protection in respect of Conviction for Offences (Article 20)

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 3.2 C

Introduction:

Article 20 of the Constitution of India deals with the Protection of certain rights in case of conviction of a person for any offence.

It grants protection against arbitrary and excessive punishment to an accused person, whether a citizen or a foreigner.

It contains three provisions:

  • Article 20 (1) deals with protection against ex-post-facto laws
  • Article 20 (2) deals with protection against double jeopardy
  • Article 20 (3) deals with protection against self-incrimination.
1. Ex-Post-Facto Laws

It means that no person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act, nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that prescribed by the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence.

The term Ex-post facto is derived from the Latin words meaning “out of the aftermath” or “retrospective effect of law.”

Protection under Article 20(1) is in the field of criminal law only and not for any civil liability and this prohibition is in respect of Substantive law and not in respect of Procedural law.

Beneficial Ex-post facto laws are not prohibited

Beneficial Ex-post facto laws are laws that apply retrospectively to provide a benefit to an accused person, such as a reduction in punishment. Such laws are not prohibited under Article 20(1) because Article 20(1) prohibits an enhanced penalty but does not prohibit a reduction in punishment.

In Rattan Lal vs. State of Punjab, AIR 1965 SC 444, the Supreme Court held that retrospective implementation of criminal laws, where an accused gets reduction in punishment is not affected by Article 20(1). Beneficial ex post facto law is not prohibited.

Only conviction or punishment is prohibited

In Mohan Lal vs. State of Rajasthan, AIR 2015 SC 2098, the Supreme Court held that only conviction and/or punishment under an ex post facto law is prohibited under Article 20(1) and not the trial or prosecution itself.

The Supreme Court also observed that trial under a different procedure than what existed during the commission of the act does not come under the ambit of the same and cannot be struck down as unconstitutional.

Procedural right of citizen

In Union of India v. Sukumar Pyne, AIR 1966 SC 1206, the Court held that Article 20(1) does not guarantee a citizen any particular court procedure as a fundamental right.

Article 20(1) does not give a citizen a fundamental right to demand that the trial follow the same procedure that existed when the offence was committed. A later law may change where the trial is held, and this change is not protected by Article 20(1).

2. Double Jeoparady / Doctroine of Double Jeoparady

The Doctrine of Double Jeopardy is given under Article 20(2) of the Constitution of India, which states that “No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.”

It is based on the common law maxim “Nemo debet bis vexari,” which means that a person must not be put twice in peril for the same offence.

Essentials of Doctroine of Double Jeoparady

  • Person must be accused of an offence which means an act or omission made punishable by law for the time being in force.
  • The proceeding or the prosecution must be taken place before a 'court' or 'judicial tribunal'.
  • The person must have been prosecuted and punished in the previous proceeding.
  • The offence must be the same for which he was prosecuted and punished in previous proceedings.

Major Cases on Double Jeoparqady

1. A.A. Mulla & Ors. v. State of Maharashtra, AIR 1997 SC 1441

Brief Explanation: The case concerns whether a second trial amounts to double jeopardy when the accused was neither convicted nor acquitted in the first trial.

Principle: If the accused was neither convicted nor acquitted on the charges in the first trial, the second trial does not amount to double jeopardy.

2. State of Mizoram v. Dr. C. Sangnghina, AIR 2018 SC 5342

Brief Explanation: The case concerns the application of double jeopardy when the accused has not actually been tried.

Principle: Where the accused has not been tried and has neither been convicted nor acquitted, the principle of double jeopardy cannot be invoked. The bar does not apply merely because the person was discharged due to a lack of evidence.

3. Kolla Veera Raghav Rao v. G. Venkateswara Rao, (2011) 2 SCC 703

Brief Explanation: The case explains the difference between the constitutional protection against double jeopardy and the protection under Section 300 CrPC.

Principle: Article 20(2) embodies the principle of autrefois convict, meaning that a person who has been prosecuted and punished for a particular offence cannot be tried again for the same offence. Section 300 CrPC also embodies the principle of autrefois acquit, meaning that a person who has been charged and subsequently acquitted cannot be tried again for the same offence, subject to applicable legal conditions.

Important Note: The supplied study material states that the book prints Article 20(1) in this context, although the principle legally refers to Article 20(2).

4. Ravinder Singh v. Sukhbir Singh & Ors., (2013) 9 SCC 245

Brief Explanation: The case explains the difference between issue estoppel and double jeopardy.

Principle: Issue estoppel is also known as cause of action estoppel and is different from double jeopardy or autrefois acquit. The parties in both trials must be the same, and the fact or issue decided in the earlier trial must be identical to the issue raised in the subsequent trial. Issue estoppel prevents the same issue from being litigated again between the parties.

3. Right against Self Incrimination

Article 20(3) states that no person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. It is based on the common law maxim "nemo tenetur prodere accussare seipsum", which means that no man is bound to accuse himself. This protection is given only if the following ingredients are present:

  • It is a protection available to a person "accused of an offence".
  • It is a protection against "compulsion to be a witness" against oneself.
  • It is a protection against such compulsion as relating to his giving evidence against himself.

Major Cases on Self Incrimination

1. M.P. Sharma v. Satish Chandra (1954)

Meaning of the Finding: The Supreme Court gave a very broad (wide) meaning to the phrase "to be a witness." The Court said that being a witness does not just mean speaking inside a courtroom. It also includes producing documents or objects out of court that can be used as evidence against the accused person.

Details of the Case: The government ordered an investigation into a company for fraud. The police got search warrants (legal permission from a magistrate) and raided several places to seize (take control of) the company's account books and records. The accused argued that raiding their offices and taking their private documents was the same as forcing them to give evidence against themselves under Article 20(3).

Decision of the Court: The Supreme Court held that forcing an accused person by a formal court order to hand over a document that proves their guilt is prohibited under Article 20(3). However, a police search and seizure (when police enter a place under a valid warrant and find documents themselves) is not an act of the accused person. Since the accused is not being forced to do anything actively during a police search, a valid search warrant does not violate Article 20(3).

Importance of this Case: This was the first landmark judgment on Article 20(3). It established two key rules: first, protection against self-incrimination includes both oral (spoken) statements and documentary (written) evidence; second, police searches and seizures under a valid warrant are completely legal and constitutional.

2. State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961)

Meaning of the Finding: An eleven-judge bench of the Supreme Court narrowed down the rule from the M.P. Sharma case. The Court made a clear difference between giving personal knowledge (mental statements) and giving physical samples for comparison.

Details of the Case: During police investigations, accused persons were asked to give their thumb impressions, palm prints, footprints, and specimen (sample) handwriting so the police could match them with evidence found at the crime scene. The accused argued that forcing them to give these samples violated Article 20(3).

Decision of the Court: The Supreme Court ruled that "to be a witness" means imparting (sharing) personal knowledge about a relevant fact through oral or written statements. Giving thumb impressions, footprints, finger impressions, or sample handwriting does not share any personal story or guilty knowledge. By themselves, these samples neither prove guilt nor innocence; they are just physical materials used for comparison by experts. The Court also clarified that compulsion (force) must mean physical threat, beating, or mental torture, not merely asking a question while a person is in police custody.

Importance of this Case: This case is extremely important for criminal investigations. It allowed the police to legally collect fingerprints, footprints, handwriting samples, and physical measurements (and later, under the Criminal Procedure (Identification) Act, 2022, biological and DNA samples) without violating Article 20(3).

3. Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani (1978)

Meaning of the Finding: The Supreme Court held that the protection of Article 20(3) is not limited to the courtroom trial. It starts much earlier, at the stage of police interrogation (questioning by the police during an investigation). It also gave the accused the Right to Silence.

Details of the Case: Nandini Satpathy, a former Chief Minister of Odisha, was called to the police station for questioning in a corruption case. She was given a long list of written questions. She refused to answer them, claiming her right under Article 20(3). The police charged her under Section 179 of the Indian Penal Code (punishment for refusing to answer a public servant's questions).

Decision of the Court: The Supreme Court protected her and ruled that a person cannot be forced to answer questions during police interrogation if the answers have a tendency (chance) to expose them to a criminal charge. Furthermore, the Court expanded the meaning of compulsion. It held that compulsion is not just physical beating; it also includes psychological or mental pressure, continuous exhausting questioning, and threatening environments inside a police station. The Court also stressed that the police must inform the accused of their right to consult a lawyer.

Importance of this Case: This judgment brought the protection of Article 20(3) directly inside the police station. It established the Right to Silence during police questioning and recognized mental torture as illegal compulsion.

4. Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010)

Meaning of the Finding: The Supreme Court ruled that forcing an accused or suspect to undergo scientific brain and lie-detector tests without their consent (free permission) violates Article 20(3) and Article 21 (Right to Personal Liberty).

Details of the Case: Investigating agencies started using modern scientific tests on suspects who refused to confess. These three tests were: 1. Narcoanalysis Test (injecting a drug called sodium pentothal so the person enters a half-asleep state and loses control over what they speak), 2. Polygraph Test (lie-detector test measuring heart rate, blood pressure, and sweating while answering questions), and 3. BEAP / Brain Mapping Test (measuring electrical brain waves to see if the suspect's brain recognizes photos or sounds from the crime scene).

Decision of the Court: The Supreme Court held that forcing a person to take these tests is unconstitutional. Unlike fingerprints or DNA (which are just physical parts of the body), these tests force a person to reveal the personal knowledge stored inside their mind without their conscious control. This is a direct form of mental compulsion and violates Article 20(3). It also violates Article 21 because invading a person's brain violates their mental privacy.

Importance of this Case: This landmark case updated Article 20(3) for the modern scientific age. It banned involuntary (forced) narcoanalysis, polygraph, and brain-mapping tests. The Court ruled that even if a person freely agrees to take the test before a Magistrate, the direct test results cannot be used as main evidence in court; only a fact or object newly discovered with the help of a voluntary statement can be admitted (under Section 27 of the Indian Evidence Act / Section 23 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam).

Summary of Findings for Quick Revision

1. M.P. Sharma (1954): "Witness" includes oral and written documents; valid police search and seizure is allowed and does not violate Article 20(3).

2. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961): Giving fingerprints, footprints, handwriting samples, or physical measurements is not self-incrimination because it does not share personal mental knowledge.

3. Nandini Satpathy (1978): Protection starts during police interrogation; accused has the Right to Silence; mental pressure counts as illegal compulsion.

4. Selvi (2010): Forced narcoanalysis, polygraph (lie-detector), and brain-mapping tests are banned as they violate mental privacy and force out personal knowledge.
