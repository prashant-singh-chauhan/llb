Module 03 · Fundamental Rights

Right to Freedom of Speech and Expression (Article 19(1)(a))

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 3.2 B

1. Article 19: All Citizens shall have the right -

Article 19(1)(a): to freedom of speech and expression.

Article 19(1)(b): to assemble peaceably and without arms.

Article 19(1)(c): to form associations, unions and co-operative societies.

Article 19(1)(d): to move freely throughout the territory of India.

Article 19(1)(e): to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India.

Article 19(1)(g): to practise any profession or to carry on any occupation, trade or business.

2. Scope of Freedom of Speech and Expression

The scope of freedom of speech and expression includes the following:

1. Freedom to Express Opinions: Every citizen has the right to express personal thoughts, opinions and ideas through speaking, writing, printing, paintings or other forms of communication. This freedom helps people share their views on social, political, economic and other matters.

2. Freedom of the Press: Freedom of the press is an important part of freedom of speech and expression. Newspapers, magazines and other news organisations have the right to publish news, opinions and information. However, the press must follow the reasonable restrictions prescribed under Article 19(2).

3. Right to Receive Information: Freedom of speech and expression also includes the right to receive information and ideas. Citizens should be able to access information and listen to different opinions. This helps them form independent views and participate meaningfully in a democratic society.

4. Freedom to Criticise the Government: Citizens have the right to peacefully criticise government policies, decisions and actions. They may express disagreement with the government and suggest changes. However, this freedom is subject to the restrictions specified in Article 19(2).

5. Freedom of Expression through the Internet and Electronic Media: Citizens may express their views through websites, social media, television, radio and other electronic platforms. The internet is an important means of communication, but online expression may be restricted by law when the requirements of Article 19(2) are satisfied.

6. Freedom of Silence: Freedom of speech and expression also includes the right not to speak or express an opinion in certain circumstances. A person cannot generally be compelled to express a view against their will. In Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986), the Supreme Court protected students who respectfully stood during the National Anthem but did not sing it because of their religious beliefs.

7. Freedom to Express Ideas through Different Mediums: Citizens may communicate their ideas through speeches, books, films, artistic works, photographs and other forms of expression. Such expression is protected, subject to lawful and reasonable restrictions under the Constitution.

Conclusion: Thus, Article 19(1)(a) protects the right to express, communicate and receive ideas through different means. It is essential for individual liberty and democracy, but it must be exercised within the constitutional limits laid down under Article 19(2).

3. Restrictions on the freedom of speech and expression

Article 19(2):Article 19(1)(a) of the Indian Constitution guarantees every citizen the Fundamental Right to freedom of speech and expression. However, this right is not absolute. Article 19(2) empowers the State to impose reasonable restrictions by law on following eight specified grounds to protect national interests, public order and the rights of others:

1. Sovereignty and Integrity of India: Restrictions may be imposed to protect the unity and integrity of India.

2. Security of the State: Restrictions may be imposed to protect the State against serious threats to its security.

3. Friendly Relations with Foreign States: Restrictions may be imposed to protect friendly relations between India and other countries.

4. Public Order: Restrictions may be imposed to maintain peace, safety and public order in society.

5. Decency or Morality: Restrictions may be imposed to protect standards of decency and morality.

6. Contempt of Court: Restrictions may be imposed in relation to contempt of court to protect the authority of the judiciary and the administration of justice.

7. Defamation: Restrictions may be imposed to protect a person's reputation against defamatory statements.

8. Incitement to an Offence: Restrictions may be imposed to prevent speech that encourages or provokes the commission of an offence.

4. Major Judicial Cases:

(A) Sovereignty and Integrity of India: The State may restrict speech that threatens the unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity of India.

Case: Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab (1995)

Facts: Two persons raised pro-Khalistan slogans after the assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi.

Judgment: The Supreme Court acquitted them because the slogans were raised casually and did not cause public disorder or incite violence.

Principle: Mere slogans or unpopular opinions do not automatically justify punishment. The circumstances and connection with the alleged threat must be examined.

(B) Security of the State: Restrictions may be imposed on speech that threatens State security, such as speech inciting violence or serious disorder.

Case: Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962)

Facts: The accused made a speech strongly criticising the government and was prosecuted under the then-existing sedition law.

Judgment: The Supreme Court upheld the provision subject to a narrow interpretation limiting its application to speech involving incitement to violence or public disorder.

Principle: Strong criticism of the government is protected unless it crosses the constitutionally relevant threshold of inciting violence or public disorder.

(C) Friendly Relations with Foreign States: The State may impose reasonable restrictions on speech that threatens friendly diplomatic relations between India and other countries. Such restrictions must be imposed by law and must genuinely relate to this constitutional ground.

(D) Public Order: Restrictions may be imposed to prevent speech that threatens public peace, safety and tranquillity.

Case: Ramji Lal Modi v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1957)

Facts: The constitutional validity of Section 295A of the Indian Penal Code, concerning deliberate and malicious insults to religious beliefs, was challenged.

Judgment: The Supreme Court upheld the provision as a reasonable restriction in the interests of public order.

Principle: Speech may be restricted when the law reasonably addresses conduct threatening public order, subject to constitutional requirements.

(E) Decency or Morality: The State may restrict obscene or indecent expression in accordance with constitutional standards.

Case: Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal (2014)

Facts: A publication reproduced a photograph of tennis player Boris Becker and his fiancée in the nude in an article discussing racial prejudice.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that the photograph was not obscene when assessed in its proper context.

Principle: Obscenity must be judged according to contemporary community standards and the overall context. Nudity alone does not automatically make material obscene.

(F) Contempt of Court: Speech may be restricted when it amounts to contempt of court and interferes with the administration of justice. However, fair criticism of judicial decisions is permitted.

Case: P.N. Duda v. V.P. Shiv Shankar (1988)

Facts: A petition sought contempt proceedings concerning a speech by the then Union Law Minister that criticised the judiciary.

Judgment: The Supreme Court declined to initiate contempt proceedings.

Principle: Fair criticism of the judiciary is protected. Contempt powers should not be used merely to silence legitimate criticism.

(G) Defamation: The State may restrict defamatory statements that unlawfully harm another person's reputation.

Case: Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India (2016)

Facts: The constitutional validity of criminal defamation provisions under Sections 499 and 500 of the Indian Penal Code was challenged.

Judgment: The Supreme Court upheld their constitutional validity.

Principle: Freedom of speech must be balanced against the right to reputation, which is protected under Article 21.

(H) Incitement to an Offence: The State may restrict speech that incites the commission of an offence.

Case: Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015)

Facts: The constitutional validity of Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, which punished broadly defined online communications, was challenged.

Judgment: The Supreme Court struck down Section 66A as unconstitutional because it was vague and excessively restricted freedom of speech.

Principle: Mere discussion or advocacy of an idea cannot automatically be punished. Restrictions must fall within the grounds permitted by Article 19(2).

5. The Expanding Horizons of Freedom of Speech and Expression: Important Judicial Innovations

Introduction:

Article 19(1)(a) of the Indian Constitution guarantees every citizen the fundamental right to freedom of speech and expression. The Supreme Court has interpreted this right broadly and extended it to different forms of communication. These judicial innovations have strengthened individual liberty, democracy and the free exchange of ideas.

1. Freedom of the Press

Case: Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950)

Facts: The Madras Government prohibited the entry and circulation of the journal Cross Roads in the State.

Judgment: The Supreme Court struck down the ban under the constitutional provisions applicable at that time.

Principle: Freedom of speech and expression includes freedom of the press and the right to circulate ideas. This established an important foundation for press freedom in India.

2. Right to Know

Case: State of Uttar Pradesh v. Raj Narain (1975)

Facts: Raj Narain sought the disclosure of government documents relating to security arrangements for the then Prime Minister.

Judgment: The Supreme Court emphasised the people's right to know about public acts performed by public officials, subject to legitimate claims of confidentiality.

Principle: The right to know is an important aspect of freedom of speech and expression. It promotes transparency and accountability in government.

3. Right to Remain Silent

Case: Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986)

Facts: Three schoolchildren respectfully stood during the National Anthem but did not sing it because of their religious beliefs. They were expelled from school.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that their expulsion violated Articles 19(1)(a) and 25 of the Constitution.

Principle: Freedom of speech and expression includes the right to remain silent. A person cannot be compelled to express something against their conscience in circumstances protected by the Constitution.

4. Commercial Speech

Case: Tata Press Ltd. v. Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Ltd. (1995)

Facts: The dispute concerned the publication of a commercial telephone directory containing advertisements for products and services.

Judgment: The Supreme Court recognised that commercial advertisements providing useful information to consumers are protected under Article 19(1)(a).

Principle: Freedom of speech includes commercial speech because consumers have a right to receive information and make informed choices.

5. Freedom of Expression through Electronic Media

Case: Secretary, Ministry of Information and Broadcasting v. Cricket Association of Bengal (1995)

Facts: The case concerned the broadcasting of cricket matches and the government's control over television broadcasting facilities.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that freedom of speech and expression includes the right to communicate and receive information through electronic media. It also recognised that airwaves are public property.

Principle: Freedom of expression extends to broadcasting and the communication of information through electronic media.

6. Symbolic Expression

Case: Union of India v. Naveen Jindal (2004)

Facts: Naveen Jindal challenged restrictions that prevented him from flying the National Flag at his factory premises.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that flying the National Flag with respect and dignity is a form of expression protected under Article 19(1)(a), subject to lawful restrictions.

Principle: Freedom of speech and expression includes symbolic expression, such as displaying the National Flag to express patriotism.

7. Freedom of Expression on the Internet

Case: Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015)

Facts: Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, criminalised certain online messages using vague and broadly worded expressions.

Judgment: The Supreme Court struck down Section 66A as unconstitutional because it violated Article 19(1)(a).

Principle: Freedom of speech extends to online communication. Vague and overbroad laws cannot be used to suppress lawful expression. The Court distinguished discussion and advocacy from incitement.

Constitutional Limitations

Freedom of speech and expression is not absolute. Under Article 19(2), the State may impose reasonable restrictions on specified grounds, including public order, defamation, security of the State, decency or morality, and sovereignty and integrity of India. Such restrictions must satisfy constitutional requirements.

Conclusion

The Supreme Court has progressively expanded the scope of Article 19(1)(a). Its important innovations include freedom of the press, the right to know, the right to remain silent, commercial speech, electronic communication, symbolic expression and online speech. These developments have strengthened democracy and individual liberty while preserving the constitutional limitations under Article 19(2).

6. Freedom of the Press under the Indian Constitution

Freedom of the press is an essential feature of democracy. It allows newspapers, magazines, television channels and digital media to publish news, express opinions and communicate information to the public. The Indian Constitution does not expressly provide a separate right to freedom of the press. However, it is protected under Article 19(1)(a), which guarantees freedom of speech and expression to all citizens.

Meaning of Freedom of the Press

Freedom of the press means the liberty to publish and circulate news, views, opinions and information without unreasonable interference. It includes the freedom to gather information, criticise government policies, report public events and express different viewpoints. The press acts as a link between the government and the people and helps citizens understand important national and social issues.

Importance of Freedom of the Press

1. Protection of Democracy: A free press strengthens democracy by informing citizens about government activities, public policies and political developments.

2. Public Awareness: Newspapers and other media educate people about their rights, duties and social problems.

3. Government Accountability: The press investigates corruption, misuse of power and other public issues. It helps people hold public authorities accountable.

4. Expression of Public Opinion: It provides a platform for citizens, writers and experts to express their views and participate in public debate.

5. Protection of Individual Liberty: A free press helps bring human rights violations and injustice to public attention.

Important Decided Cases

1. Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950)

Facts: The Madras Government prohibited the entry and circulation of the journal Cross Roads in the State.

Judgment: The Supreme Court struck down the ban under the constitutional provisions applicable at that time.

Principle: Freedom of speech and expression includes freedom of the press and the right to circulate ideas. Free political discussion is essential for democracy.

2. Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi (1950)

Facts: The government imposed pre-censorship requirements on the newspaper Organiser.

Judgment: The Supreme Court struck down the pre-censorship order under the constitutional provisions applicable at that time.

Principle: Freedom of the press includes protection against prior restraint on publication, subject to constitutional requirements.

3. Sakal Papers (P) Ltd. v. Union of India (1962)

Facts: The government introduced rules regulating newspaper prices and the number of pages newspapers could publish.

Judgment: The Supreme Court declared the restrictions unconstitutional.

Principle: The government cannot unreasonably interfere with newspaper publication and circulation in violation of Article 19(1)(a).

4. Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India (1972)

Facts: Newspaper publishers challenged a newsprint policy that restricted the number of pages and the expansion of newspapers.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that the policy violated freedom of speech and expression.

Principle: Freedom of the press includes the right to publish news and views and to circulate newspapers effectively.

5. Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India (1985)

Facts: Newspaper publishers challenged customs duties imposed on newsprint, arguing that these duties placed a financial burden on newspapers.

Judgment: The Supreme Court examined the duties in the context of press freedom and recognised the importance of protecting the press from measures that excessively burden its functioning.

Principle: The press plays a vital role in democracy, and measures affecting its economic position must be examined in light of freedom of expression.

Conclusion

Freedom of the press is an important part of Article 19(1)(a) of the Indian Constitution. It promotes democracy, spreads awareness, encourages public debate and helps maintain government accountability. Through landmark judgments, the Supreme Court has recognised and protected the essential role of the press. A free press is therefore necessary for an informed public, responsible government and the effective functioning of Indian democracy.

7. Right to Know

Meaning: The Right to Know is an important part of the Right to Freedom of Speech and Expression guaranteed under Article 19(1)(a) of the Indian Constitution. It means that citizens have the right to receive information about public affairs and the activities of the government. The press plays an important role in exercising this right by collecting and communicating information to the public.

1. State of Uttar Pradesh v. Raj Narain (1975)

Facts: The case concerned a demand for the disclosure of government documents relating to security arrangements for the then Prime Minister.

Judgment: The Supreme Court recognised that people have a right to know about public acts performed by public officials, subject to legitimate claims of confidentiality.

Principle: The Right to Know is an important aspect of freedom of speech and expression. It promotes transparency and accountability in government.

2. Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002)

Facts: The case concerned whether voters had a right to know important details about candidates contesting elections.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that voters have a fundamental right to receive relevant information about candidates, including their criminal antecedents, assets, liabilities and educational qualifications.

Principle: The Right to Know enables citizens to make informed decisions and strengthens democracy.

Importance of the Right to Know: The Right to Know strengthens freedom of the press because a free press helps citizens obtain reliable information, understand government policies and hold public authorities accountable. The Right to Information Act, 2005, also provides a legal framework for citizens to seek information from public authorities, subject to the applicable provisions and exemptions.

8. Restrictions on Freedom of Speech and Expression on the Grounds of Obscenity and Contempt of Court

Introduction:

Article 19(1)(a) of the Indian Constitution guarantees every citizen the Right to Freedom of Speech and Expression. However, this right is not absolute. Under Article 19(2), the State may impose reasonable restrictions on specified grounds, including decency or morality and contempt of court. These restrictions aim to protect public morality and the proper administration of justice.

1. Restriction on the Ground of Obscenity

Meaning: Obscenity refers to material that violates accepted standards of decency or morality. However, every depiction of nudity, sexuality or controversial subject matter is not automatically obscene. The material must be assessed in its proper context.

Case 1: Ranjit D. Udeshi v. State of Maharashtra (1965)

Facts: A bookseller was prosecuted for selling the novel Lady Chatterley's Lover, which was alleged to be obscene.

Judgment: The Supreme Court upheld the conviction and recognised that restrictions on obscene publications could be justified in the interests of decency and morality.

Principle: Obscene material may be restricted under Article 19(2). However, sexual content in literature or art is not automatically obscene.

Case 2: Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal (2014)

Facts: A complaint alleged that the publication of a nude photograph of tennis player Boris Becker with his fiancée was obscene.

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that the photograph was not obscene when considered in its proper context and according to contemporary community standards.

Principle: Obscenity must be judged according to contemporary community standards. The material should be examined as a whole rather than by considering isolated portions.

Case 3: Bobby Art International v. Om Pal Singh Hoon (1996)

Facts: A petition challenged the exhibition of the film Bandit Queen because it contained scenes of nudity and sexual violence.

Judgment: The Supreme Court permitted the film to be exhibited with its approved adult certification, considering the disputed scenes in the context of the film's overall theme.

Principle: Nudity or sexual violence shown for a genuine artistic or social purpose is not necessarily obscene. The entire work must be considered in its proper context.

2. Restriction on the Ground of Contempt of Court

Meaning: Contempt of court refers to acts or publications that obstruct the administration of justice, interfere with judicial proceedings or undermine the authority of courts. Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions on freedom of expression to protect the administration of justice.

Case 4: E.M.S. Namboodiripad v. T.N. Nambiar (1970)

Facts: The then Chief Minister of Kerala made public statements describing the judiciary as an instrument of oppression and alleging that judges favoured wealthy people over the poor.

Judgment: The Supreme Court upheld his conviction for contempt of court, considering the statements capable of undermining public confidence in the judiciary.

Principle: Freedom of speech does not protect statements that seriously undermine judicial authority or interfere with the administration of justice.

Case 5: P.N. Duda v. V.P. Shiv Shankar (1988)

Facts: A petition sought contempt proceedings against the then Union Law Minister for a speech criticising the judiciary.

Judgment: The Supreme Court declined to initiate contempt proceedings after considering the speech as a whole and in its proper context.

Principle: Fair and reasonable criticism of judges and judicial decisions is permitted. Contempt law must not be used merely to suppress legitimate criticism of the judiciary.

Extent of Restrictions

The courts must maintain a balance between freedom of expression, public morality and the administration of justice. Under Section 13 of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, punishment for contempt requires substantial interference, or a substantial tendency to interfere, with the due course of justice. Truth may also be accepted as a defence when the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Conclusion

Freedom of speech and expression may be restricted on the grounds of obscenity and contempt of court, but such restrictions must remain within Article 19(2). Obscene material may be restricted to protect decency and morality, while expression that substantially interferes with justice may attract contempt proceedings. At the same time, artistic expression and fair criticism of judicial decisions deserve constitutional protection. The objective is to maintain a proper balance between individual liberty and the public interest.
