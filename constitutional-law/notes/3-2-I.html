Module 03 · Fundamental Rights

Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 3.2 I

Constitutional Remedies under Article 32 and the Enforcement of Fundamental Rights

Introduction: Fundamental Rights are guaranteed under Part III of the Indian Constitution. Article 32 provides the right to approach the Supreme Court when a Fundamental Right is violated. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the “heart and soul” of the Constitution because it makes Fundamental Rights enforceable through courts.

1. Article 32 as a Fundamental Right

Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right. It guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court can issue appropriate directions, orders and writs to protect these rights. Thus, Article 32 makes the protection of Fundamental Rights effective and meaningful.

2. Who Can Approach the Supreme Court?

Any person whose Fundamental Right has been violated can approach the Supreme Court under Article 32. In appropriate cases, a public-spirited person may also file a Public Interest Litigation (PIL) to protect the rights of poor, disadvantaged or vulnerable people. Fundamental Rights are generally enforced against the State and its authorities, although certain rights also apply against private individuals.

3. Five Constitutional Writs

(i) Habeas Corpus: It means “to produce the body”. The court orders the authority or person concerned to produce a detained person and examines whether the detention is lawful. It protects personal liberty.

(ii) Mandamus: It means “we command”. The court directs a public authority to perform a legal duty that it has failed or refused to perform.

(iii) Prohibition: The court orders a lower court or tribunal to stop proceedings when it acts beyond its legal authority.

(iv) Certiorari: The court may quash an unlawful order passed by a lower court, tribunal or other authority when the legal conditions for issuing the writ are satisfied.

(v) Quo Warranto: It means “by what authority”. The court examines whether a person is legally entitled to hold a public office.

4. Other Remedies and the Role of Courts

Under Article 32, the Supreme Court can also issue suitable directions and orders to protect Fundamental Rights. In appropriate cases, it may award compensation for a serious violation of Fundamental Rights. High Courts also have the power to issue writs under Article 226. Their jurisdiction is wider because they can enforce Fundamental Rights as well as other legal rights.

5. Important Decided Cases

Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950): The Supreme Court described itself as the protector and guarantor of Fundamental Rights. It held that a person could approach the Supreme Court directly under Article 32.

Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner (1963): The Court recognised that the right to approach the Supreme Court under Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right.

Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): The Court adopted a flexible approach to protect bonded labourers and strengthened the role of Public Interest Litigation in enforcing Fundamental Rights.

Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993): The Supreme Court recognised that compensation could be awarded in appropriate cases involving a violation of the right to life under Article 21.

Conclusion: Article 32 is an essential safeguard of Indian democracy. Through writs, directions, orders and other appropriate remedies, the Supreme Court protects Fundamental Rights and prevents their unlawful violation. Therefore, it rightly acts as a watchful sentinel of Fundamental Rights.
