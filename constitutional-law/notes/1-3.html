Module 01 · Nature of the Indian Constitution

Changing Dynamics

Constitutional Law of India – I · Topic 1.3

1. Indian Constitution Shifted from Competitive to Cooperative Federalism.
1. Introduction

Federalism means the division of powers between the Central Government and State Governments. Article 1 of the Indian Constitution describes India as a “Union of States.” K.C. Wheare described the Indian Constitution as quasi-federal because it establishes a strong Centre. Over time, Indian federalism has increasingly moved towards cooperative federalism. However, competitive federalism also continues to play an important role. Therefore, I largely agree with the statement.

2. Meaning of Cooperative and Competitive Federalism

Cooperative Federalism: It means that the Centre and States work together to solve common problems and promote national development.

Competitive Federalism: It means that States compete with one another to attract investment, improve public services, create employment and achieve better economic growth.

3. Constitutional Provisions Supporting Cooperation

The Constitution provides several provisions that encourage cooperation:

1. Article 246 and Seventh Schedule: They divide legislative powers between the Centre and States through the Union List, State List and Concurrent List.

2. Article 263: It provides for an Inter-State Council to discuss matters of common interest and improve coordination.

3. Article 280: It provides for the Finance Commission, which recommends the distribution of financial resources between the Centre and States.

4. Article 279A: It establishes the Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council, which brings the Centre and States together to decide GST-related matters.

5. Article 312: It provides for All India Services, such as the IAS and IPS, which help maintain administrative coordination between the Centre and States.

These provisions show that cooperation has been an important feature of Indian federalism since the beginning.

4. Evidence of the Shift Towards Cooperative Federalism

(a) Establishment of NITI Aayog: In 2015, NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission. It promotes cooperation through its Governing Council, which includes the Prime Minister and Chief Ministers. It encourages States to share ideas and work together as “Team India”.

(b) GST Council: The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016, introduced GST and established the GST Council under Article 279A. It provides a common platform for the Centre and States to discuss tax policies. In Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022), the Supreme Court recognised that GST Council recommendations have persuasive value and emphasised cooperative federalism.

(c) Finance Commission: The 14th Finance Commission recommended increasing the States’ share of the divisible pool of central taxes to 42%. This strengthened financial decentralisation and State autonomy.

(d) Judicial Recognition: In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court recognised federalism as a basic feature of the Constitution. In State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977), the Court examined the constitutional relationship between the Centre and States.

5. Role of Competitive Federalism

Competitive federalism encourages States to improve their performance. States such as Gujarat and Rajasthan have organised investment summits to attract industries. NITI Aayog also publishes indices, including the SDG India Index and Health Index, to compare State performance. Such competition promotes innovation, efficiency and better governance.

6. Challenges

Despite these developments, disputes continue over financial resources, centrally sponsored schemes, GST compensation and the distribution of powers. Political differences and water-sharing disputes also weaken cooperation. Excessive central control may turn cooperative federalism into coercive federalism.

7. Conclusion

I agree that Indian federalism has increasingly moved towards cooperation through NITI Aayog, the GST Council and constitutional institutions. However, competitive federalism has not disappeared. India follows a combination of cooperative and competitive federalism. The Centre and States must respect each other’s powers, consult one another and distribute resources fairly to ensure balanced national development.

2. The Keynote for any scheme of revision of the Indian federal system must be as, "autonomy for the states consistent with the integrity of the country". Do you agree? Discuss with reasons.
1. Introduction

Yes, I agree with this statement. It means that States should enjoy sufficient freedom to govern themselves while maintaining the unity and integrity of India. Article 1 of the Indian Constitution describes India as a “Union of States.” The Constitution provides a strong Centre to protect national unity while recognising the importance of State autonomy. Therefore, a proper balance between State autonomy and national integrity is necessary.

2. Importance of State Autonomy

(a) Protection of Diversity: India has different languages, cultures, traditions and geographical conditions. State autonomy allows State governments to make policies according to local needs and protect regional identities.

(b) Better Administration: State governments are better placed to address local problems relating to public health, police, agriculture and local government. Greater autonomy improves administrative efficiency and accountability.

(c) Democratic Governance: State governments are elected by the people. Excessive centralisation weakens democracy and reduces the freedom of elected State governments to perform their constitutional duties.

(d) Economic Development: States need sufficient financial resources to develop infrastructure, provide welfare services, attract investment and create employment according to their priorities.

(e) Regional Aspirations: Constitutional provisions such as Articles 371A to 371J and the Sixth Schedule provide special arrangements for certain regions. These arrangements help accommodate regional needs within the Indian Union.

3. Importance of National Integrity

(a) National Security: Defence, foreign affairs and national security require strong coordination by the Union Government. A strong Centre is necessary to protect India against external threats and internal security challenges.

(b) Constitutional Unity: Article 1 establishes India as a Union of States, while Article 3 empowers Parliament to form new States and alter their areas, boundaries and names. These provisions help maintain the constitutional unity of the country.

(c) Economic Integration: A common market, national transport networks and coordinated economic policies require cooperation between the Centre and States.

(d) Prevention of Disintegration: State autonomy does not mean complete independence or a right to separate from India. Every State must exercise its powers within the Constitution and respect national unity.

4. Constitutional Balance Between the Centre and States

The Constitution maintains this balance through several provisions:

1. Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule: They divide legislative powers between the Union, State and Concurrent Lists.

2. Article 263: It provides for an Inter-State Council to promote coordination between the Centre and States.

3. Article 280: It provides for the Finance Commission, which recommends the distribution of financial resources.

4. Article 356: It permits President’s Rule when a State government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. However, this power must not be misused for political purposes.

The Sarkaria Commission and Punchhi Commission also examined Centre-State relations and recommended measures to improve federal cooperation.

5. Measures for Better Federalism

The Centre should consult States on matters affecting their interests. Financial resources should be distributed fairly, the Inter-State Council should function effectively, and Governors should act according to constitutional principles. Emergency provisions must be used cautiously. States should also strengthen local self-government through Panchayats and Municipalities.

6. Conclusion

I agree that State autonomy must be maintained consistently with the integrity of the country. Excessive centralisation weakens federalism, while unlimited State independence may threaten national unity. Therefore, India needs cooperative federalism based on mutual respect, consultation, fair financial distribution and constitutional responsibility. This balance will protect regional interests while preserving the unity and integrity of India.

3. The Concept of Federalism Is Subject to Change with Changing Time. Where Will You Place the Constitution of India in This Changing Concept?
The concept of federalism changes according to the political, social and economic needs of a country. Traditional federalism emphasises the separate powers of the Centre and States, whereas modern federalism focuses on cooperation and coordination. The Indian Constitution represents a unique form of federalism that balances national unity with regional autonomy.

Federal Features of the Indian Constitution
1. Two Levels of Government:

There are two levels of government, generally the Central Government and State Governments. Both govern the same citizens but perform different functions.

2. Written Constitution:

A written Constitution clearly defines the powers, duties and responsibilities of each level of government.

3. Division of Powers:

The Constitution divides powers between the Central and State Governments. In India, these powers are divided through the Union List, State List and Concurrent List under the Seventh Schedule.

4. Supremacy of the Constitution:

The Constitution is the supreme law of the country. Both levels of government must work according to its provisions.

5. Rigidity of the Constitution:

The Constitution cannot be amended easily. Certain amendments affecting the federal system require approval by at least half of the State Legislatures.

6. Independent Judiciary:

The Supreme Court interprets the Constitution, settles disputes between the Centre and States, and protects the constitutional division of powers.

7. Financial Independence:

Both levels of government need sufficient financial resources to perform their duties. Tax-sharing arrangements help maintain financial balance.

8. Bicameral Legislature:

Many federal countries have two houses of Parliament. In India, the Rajya Sabha represents the States and Union Territories at the national level.

Unitary Features of the Indian Constitution
1. Strong Central Government

The Centre has wider powers than the States in several important matters. The Union List also contains subjects of national importance.

2. Single Citizenship

India provides for single citizenship. Every Indian is a citizen of India, and there is no separate state citizenship.

3. Appointment of Governors

Governors are appointed by the President. They serve as constitutional heads of the States and have certain constitutional responsibilities.

4. Emergency Provisions

Articles 352, 356 and 360 provide for national, state and financial emergencies respectively. These provisions can increase the powers of the Centre, subject to constitutional requirements.

5. Power to Change State Boundaries

Under Article 3, Parliament can form new States and alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing States. The views of the affected State Legislature must be sought, but its consent is not binding on Parliament.

6. Residuary Powers

Under Article 248 and Entry 97 of the Union List, Parliament has the power to legislate on subjects not mentioned in the State List or Concurrent List.

7. Integrated Judiciary and All India Services

India has an integrated judicial system, with the Supreme Court at the top and High Courts and subordinate courts below it. Services such as the IAS and IPS also help maintain administrative coordination between the Centre and the States.

4. Changing Concept of Federalism

Over time, federalism has developed beyond the traditional division of powers. In India, its changing nature can be understood through the following forms.

Cooperative Federalism: It means that the Centre and States work together to solve common problems and promote national development.

Competitive Federalism: It means that States compete with one another to attract investment, improve public services, create employment and achieve better economic growth.

Fiscal Federalism: The Finance Commission recommends how certain tax revenues should be distributed between the Centre and the States.

Decentralisation: The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments strengthened local self-government through Panchayats and Municipalities.

Changing Political Relations: The growth of regional parties and coalition governments has increased the importance of State Governments in national politics.

However, concerns remain regarding excessive centralisation and the financial autonomy of States. Effective federalism requires consultation, mutual respect and a fair distribution of resources.

5. Judicial Pronouncements and Position of India

K.C. Wheare described the Indian Constitution as “quasi-federal” because it contains federal features but gives greater powers to the Central Government. The Constitution divides powers between the Centre and States, but the Centre has special powers during emergencies.

Granville Austin emphasised the importance of cooperative federalism in India. According to this approach, the Centre and States should work together to achieve national development while respecting each other’s constitutional powers.

In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court held that federalism is a part of the basic structure of the Constitution. The judgment also restricted the misuse of Article 356, which provides for President’s Rule in a State. It reinforced the principle that State governments cannot be dismissed arbitrarily by the Centre.

6. Conclusion

The Indian Constitution reflects the changing concept of federalism. It combines federal principles with central authority to protect national unity while recognising regional needs. Its success depends on cooperation, constitutional respect and a fair balance of powers. Therefore, India can best be described as a quasi-federal state with a strong Centre that increasingly relies on cooperative and competitive federalism.

4. Case :S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

Citation: AIR 1994 SC 1918; (1994) 3 SCC 1

Court: Supreme Court of India

Date of Judgment: 11 March 1994

Bench: Nine-Judge Constitution Bench

1. Facts of the Case

S.R. Bommai was the Chief Minister of Karnataka and headed a Janata Dal government in 1988. In 1989, several members of the Legislative Assembly reportedly withdrew their support from his government. This created doubts about whether his government continued to enjoy a majority in the Assembly.

Bommai requested the Governor to call a meeting of the Legislative Assembly so that he could prove his majority on the floor of the House. However, the Governor did not provide him that opportunity and recommended the imposition of President's Rule.

On 21 April 1989, President's Rule was imposed in Karnataka under Article 356 of the Constitution, and Bommai's government was dismissed.

Bommai challenged this decision. He argued that the Governor should have allowed him to prove his majority in the Legislative Assembly. Similar challenges concerning the dismissal of State Governments in other states were also considered by the Supreme Court.

2. Main Legal Issues
  1. Whether the President's decision under Article 356 was subject to judicial review.
  2. Whether the Union Government could dismiss an elected State Government without sufficient constitutional grounds.
  3. Whether a Chief Minister's majority should be determined through a floor test.
  4. Whether federalism and secularism are part of the basic structure of the Constitution.
3. Judgment of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court delivered its landmark judgment on 11 March 1994 and established the following principles:

  • Judicial Review: A proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review.
  • Floor Test: The majority of a government should ordinarily be determined on the floor of the Legislative Assembly.
  • Federalism: Federalism is an essential feature of the basic structure of the Constitution.
  • Secularism: Secularism is also a part of the basic structure of the Constitution.
  • Limits on Article 356: The Union Government cannot arbitrarily dismiss an elected State Government.
  • Restoration of Government: If a proclamation is declared unconstitutional, the court may grant appropriate relief, including restoration of the dismissed government.
4. Importance of the Case

This judgment placed important limits on the use of President's Rule and strengthened Indian federalism. It protected elected State Governments against arbitrary dismissal and reinforced the principle that constitutional powers must be exercised according to the law.

5. Conclusion

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) is a landmark judgment in Indian constitutional law. It strengthened federalism, protected democratic government in the States and established that the exercise of power under Article 356 is subject to judicial review.

Important Points for Examination
  • Article 356 — President's Rule in States.
  • Judicial review of the President's Proclamation.
  • Floor test to determine legislative majority.
  • Federalism and secularism as features of the basic structure.
