Introduction

Jurisprudence · Topic 5.1

1. Introduction

The Sociological School of Jurisprudence is an important school of legal thought that studies the relationship between law and society. It explains that law is not merely a collection of rules made by the government; rather, it is a social institution that helps maintain peace, justice, order, and social welfare. According to this school, law should be understood in relation to the needs, problems, and changing conditions of society. Its main purpose is to serve society, protect people's interests, and promote social welfare.

The Sociological School of Jurisprudence developed as a response to the limitations of earlier schools of legal thought, which mainly focused on the origin, principles, and logical interpretation of law. These approaches did not always give sufficient importance to the actual relationship between law and society. Therefore, the Sociological School introduced a practical approach, emphasising that law should be studied by examining its effects on society and its ability to solve real-life social problems. It believes that laws should change according to the needs of society and help establish a fair, peaceful, and progressive social order.

Characteristics of the Sociological School
1. Law and Society Are Interrelated

The school considers law and society closely connected. Laws develop from social needs and influence the behaviour of people.

2. Emphasis on Social Welfare

The main objective of law should be to promote public welfare, justice, and social harmony rather than protect only the interests of a few individuals.

3. Practical Approach

This school studies how laws actually work in society. It examines whether laws achieve their objectives and solve real-life social problems.

4. Law Is Dynamic

Society changes over time, so laws must also develop. Rules that were suitable in the past may not be suitable for modern social conditions.

5. Balancing of Interests

Roscoe Pound explained that law should balance individual interests, public interests, and social interests. This helps reduce conflicts and promote justice.

6. Importance of Social Facts

The school gives importance to social conditions, customs, economic circumstances, and human behaviour while studying legal rules.

7. Law as an Instrument of Social Change

Law can help remove harmful social practices and promote positive changes. For example, laws against untouchability, child labour, and discrimination aim to improve society.

8. Importance of Living Law

Eugen Ehrlich argued that the rules followed by people in everyday life are important for understanding how society is actually regulated. This concept is known as living law.

Difference Between Analytical School and Sociological School
Basis Analytical School Sociological School
1. Meaning Studies law as it exists in the form of legal rules. Studies the relationship between law and society.
2. Main Thinker John Austin is its main representative. Roscoe Pound is one of its main representatives.
3. Nature of Law Views law mainly as a command of the sovereign backed by sanctions. Views law as a social institution that responds to social needs.
4. Main Focus Focuses on the nature, validity, and structure of legal rules. Focuses on social welfare and the effects of law on society.
5. Purpose To explain and analyse the nature of positive law. To promote justice and improve society through law.
6. Relationship with Society Gives less importance to social conditions when defining law. Gives great importance to social conditions and human behaviour.
7. Method of Study Uses analytical and logical methods to study legal rules. Uses practical and sociological methods to study law.
8. Role of the State Emphasises the authority of the sovereign in making law. Emphasises the role of law in meeting social needs and balancing interests.
9. Change in Law Examines the validity and structure of existing law. Emphasises that law should respond to changing social conditions.
10. Important Concept Law as the command of the sovereign. Law as an instrument of social engineering.
11. Example Examines the legal validity of a law enacted by the legislature. Examines whether a law effectively solves a social problem.
12. Approach Primarily concerned with law as it is. Concerned with law as it operates in society and how it can improve social conditions.
