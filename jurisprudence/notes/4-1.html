Module 04 · Historical School

Introduction

Jurisprudence · Topic 4.1

Introduction · Meaning of Historical School · Features · Difference from Analytical School

1. Introduction

The Historical School of Jurisprudence studies law in relation to its historical origin, social development, customs, traditions, and the life of the people. It believes that law is not merely created by the State but develops gradually with the growth of society.

According to this school, law is closely connected with the history, culture, beliefs, and common consciousness of a community. It develops over time through social practices, customs, traditions, and institutions.

Friedrich Carl von Savigny is regarded as the principal representative of the Historical School. Other important jurists include Sir Henry Maine, Georg Friedrich Puchta, and Montesquieu, whose ideas contributed to historical and sociological approaches to law.

The central idea of this school is that law must be understood through the historical development of the society in which it operates.

2. Meaning of the Historical School

The Historical School of Jurisprudence is a school of legal philosophy that explains law through its origin, evolution, and development in society. It rejects the idea that law can be fully understood merely by examining commands issued by a sovereign or rules formally enacted by the State.

According to this approach, law develops gradually from the customs, traditions, and collective experiences of the people. It reflects the historical circumstances and social consciousness of a community.

Savigny explained this idea through the concept of Volksgeist, a German term meaning the spirit of the people. According to him, law grows naturally from the common consciousness of a nation and is expressed through its customs, institutions, and legal traditions.

Sir Henry Maine further contributed to historical jurisprudence by examining the development of legal institutions and the movement of societies from status-based relationships towards relationships increasingly based on contract.

3. Salient Features of the Historical School
1. Historical Origin and Development of Law

The Historical School believes that law must be understood by examining its origin and historical development. It studies how legal rules and institutions emerge, change, and develop over time.

Instead of examining only the present form of law, this school investigates the historical circumstances that influenced its formation.

2. Law as a Product of Society

According to the Historical School, law is closely connected with the life and development of society. It emerges from the experiences, customs, traditions, and practices of the people.

Law is therefore not viewed merely as an artificial creation of the State. It develops alongside the social institutions and relationships of a community.

3. Savigny's Theory of Volksgeist

One of the most important features of the Historical School is Savigny's theory of Volksgeist, meaning the spirit or common consciousness of the people.

According to Savigny, law originates in the common consciousness of a nation and develops naturally through its customs, traditions, and social institutions. Law expresses the historical identity and collective life of the people.

Savigny therefore opposed the idea that a complete legal system could simply be imposed upon a nation without regard to its history and social conditions.

4. Importance of Custom

The Historical School gives great importance to custom as a source of law. Customs arise through repeated social practices followed by members of a community over a long period.

When customs become established and are recognised as legally binding, they may develop into rules of customary law.

For example, certain customary practices relating to family relationships, inheritance, and community affairs have historically influenced the development of legal rules.

5. Organic Growth of Law

The Historical School considers law to be something that develops gradually and organically with society. It is not always the result of deliberate legislation or a single legislative decision.

Legal institutions change as social conditions, economic activities, political arrangements, and community practices evolve. Law must therefore be studied as a developing institution.

6. Importance of Legal Traditions and Institutions

The school recognises the importance of traditions, social institutions, and established practices in shaping legal systems.

Family structures, property relationships, inheritance practices, and community institutions have historically influenced the development of law. Understanding these institutions helps explain why different societies may develop different legal rules.

7. Opposition to Arbitrary Legal Reform

Savigny believed that legal reform should take account of the history, customs, and social conditions of a nation. Sudden or artificial changes that ignore these factors may fail to reflect the needs of the community.

However, this does not mean that the Historical School rejects legislation or every form of legal reform. Its central concern is that legal changes should be informed by historical understanding and the actual conditions of society.

8. Law Reflects the Character of a Nation

According to the Historical School, the legal system of a nation reflects its historical experiences, cultural traditions, social relationships, and collective consciousness.

Since societies have different histories and traditions, their legal systems may develop differently. A legal rule that works in one society may not be equally suitable for another without considering its social and historical context.

9. Maine's Theory of Status to Contract

Sir Henry Maine made an important contribution to historical jurisprudence through his analysis of the development of legal relationships.

In his work Ancient Law (1861), Maine famously observed that the movement of progressive societies has generally been from status to contract.

Status refers to a person's legal position determined largely by birth, family, or social position. Contract refers to relationships increasingly formed through individual agreement and choice.

For example, in traditional societies, a person's rights and obligations may depend heavily on family position or inherited social status. In modern legal systems, many commercial and employment relationships are increasingly regulated through agreements between individuals.

Maine's proposition describes a broad historical tendency rather than a universal rule that applies identically to every society or legal relationship.

10. Law and Social Change

The Historical School recognises that law changes as society develops. Changes in economic conditions, political institutions, technology, and social practices may gradually influence legal rules.

Studying these changes helps jurists understand why certain laws emerge, why older rules lose importance, and how legal institutions adapt to new circumstances.

Critical Analysis

The Historical School has several merits. It highlights the importance of custom, tradition, social experience, and historical continuity in the development of law. It helps explain why legal systems differ across societies and why legal rules cannot always be understood independently of their social context.

It also provides a valuable method for understanding customary law, legal institutions, and the historical development of legal principles. Savigny's theory encouraged jurists to examine the social and cultural foundations of law rather than treating legislation as the only significant source of legal rules.

However, the school also has limitations. An excessive emphasis on historical traditions may discourage necessary legal reform. Customs may preserve practices that are unjust, discriminatory, or inconsistent with modern principles of equality and individual rights.

Furthermore, the theory of Volksgeist can be difficult to define precisely, particularly in diverse societies where different communities have different customs and values. The school may also understate the importance of deliberate legislation and the role of political institutions in changing the law.

Therefore, historical development is an important factor in understanding law, but it should be considered alongside justice, social needs, constitutional principles, and the requirements of modern legal systems.

Conclusion

The Historical School is an important school of jurisprudence that explains law through its historical origin and gradual development in society. Its major features include the importance of custom, the organic growth of law, Savigny's theory of Volksgeist, and Maine's analysis of the movement from status to contract.

Although the school may place excessive emphasis on tradition and historical continuity, it provides a valuable understanding of the relationship between law and society. It demonstrates that the development of legal systems cannot be fully understood without examining their historical and social foundations.

4. Difference Between Historical and Analytical School
# Basis Historical School Analytical School
1 Main Thinkers Friedrich Carl von Savigny and Sir Henry Maine Jeremy Bentham and John Austin
2 Main Idea Law is understood through its historical development. Law is studied as it exists in a legal system.
3 Source of Law Emphasises customs, traditions, and social practices. Emphasises positive law and legally recognised rules.
4 View of Law Law develops gradually with society. Studies the nature and structure of existing law.
5 Method Historical, evolutionary, and contextual. Analytical, conceptual, and logical.
6 Custom Custom is an important source of law and reflects social development. Its status depends on the relevant legal theory; Austin emphasises sovereign recognition.
7 State Emphasises the historical and social foundations of legal rules. Gives importance to positive law and legal authority, especially in Austin's theory.
8 Legislation Should take account of historical development and social traditions. Is an important means of creating and modifying positive law.
9 Development of Law Focuses on the origin, growth, and evolution of legal institutions. Focuses mainly on the meaning, structure, and validity of existing law.
10 Important Concept Savigny's Volksgeist, meaning the spirit of the people. Austin's Command Theory of Law.
11 Nature of Law Law develops organically through the historical life of society. Law is examined as positive law established or recognised within a legal system.
12 Scope Studies the historical development of customs, legal institutions, and legal systems. Studies legal concepts, rules, and the structure of existing legal systems.
