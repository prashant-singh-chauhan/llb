Definition

Jurisprudence · Topic 1.1

Introduction

The study of jurisprudence started with the Romans. The term Jurisprudence is derived from the Latin word 'Jurisprudentia' which means either "Knowledge of Law" or "Skill of law". The word "juris" means law and “prudentia” means knowledge, science or skill.

Jurisprudence does not study only the rules of a particular law. It studies the basic principles, concepts, nature and purpose of law. Therefore, it is often called the philosophy or theory of law.

It does not mean study of statutes or branches of law like consumer, criminal, tort, property, etc., but the basic principles behind the development of these statutes and branches of law.

Definition

It is very difficult to define the term 'Jurisprudence', however, several attempts have been made in this context to define the term. Some of the definitions of the term "jurisprudence" given by various eminent jurists as under –

1. Ulpian

Jurisprudence is the “knowledge of things divine and human, the science of the just and unjust.”

2. John Austin

Jurisprudence is the philosophy of positive law.

3. Holland

Jurisprudence is the formal science of positive law.

4. Salmond

Jurisprudence is the science of the first principles of the civil law.

5. C.K. Allen

Jurisprudence is the scientific synthesis of the essential principles of law.

6. Julius Stone

Jurisprudence is lawyer’s extraversion

Which Definition is Most Appropriate?

In my opinion, Salmond’s definition is more appropriate for understanding jurisprudence.

According to Salmond:
“Jurisprudence is the science of the first principles of the civil law.”

Salmond's definition has three main parts.

First, jurisprudence is a "science", which means it is a systematic body of knowledge, not just a collection of rules.

Second, it deals with "first principles", meaning the basic concepts and principles of law, such as rights, duties, possession, ownership, and personality.

Third, it concerns "civil law", which means the law of the state as applied by the courts.

Why Salmond's Definition is More Appropriate

1. It is wider and more flexible.

It covers the foundational ideas of law rather than only the commands of a sovereign. This makes it useful for studying different legal systems and different schools of thought, such as analytical, historical, and ethical jurisprudence. Austin's definition, on the other hand, is narrow because it focuses only on command, sovereign, and sanction.

2. It matches how lawyers and judges actually work.

When judges decide cases, they do not simply look for a command from a sovereign. They reason from general principles such as justice, fairness, rights, and duties. Salmond's focus on "first principles" reflects this real-life legal reasoning.

3. It gives importance to the role of the courts.

According to him, law is what the courts recognise and apply. This is more practical than Austin's theory, because many rules become law through judicial decisions and not only through legislation.

4. It accepts the connection between law and morality.

He included ethical jurisprudence as a branch of study. Austin separated law from morality completely, which makes his definition less helpful for understanding concepts like justice and natural law.
