Module 02 · Natural Law School

Revival of Natural Law

Jurisprudence · Topic 2.2

Introduction · Decline · Revival · Features ·

1. Introduction

Natural law is an old theory which says that law must be based on reason, morality and justice. It believes that there is a higher law above man-made law. In the 19th century, natural law lost its importance because of the rise of positivism and the historical school. However, in the 20th century, it again became popular. This comeback is called the revival of natural law.

2. Reasons for the Decline of Natural Law

Natural Law declined during the 19th century due to the rise of other schools of legal thought.

1. Analytical School: John Austin argued that law is the command of the sovereign backed by sanctions. He separated law from morality and focused on laws made by the State.

2. Historical School: Friedrich Carl von Savigny argued that law develops from the customs, traditions, and spirit of the people. He gave importance to historical development rather than universal principles of Natural Law.

3. Rise of Legal Positivism: Thinkers began to focus on positive law, meaning laws made and recognised by the State, rather than on moral principles or higher law.

4. Growth of Scientific Thinking: The growing importance of science and logical analysis encouraged scholars to study law as it actually exists instead of debating what law ought to be.

Conclusion: Due to these developments, Natural Law lost importance during the 19th century and was largely overshadowed by positivist and historical approaches to law.

3. Reasons for the Revival of Natural Law

Natural Law was revived in the 20th century because of several political, social, and legal developments. The two World Wars, the growth of human rights, and the need to control the arbitrary power of the State increased the importance of Natural Law.

1. Impact of the Two World Wars

The First and Second World Wars showed that laws made by the State could be unjust and inhuman. During Nazi rule in Germany, laws were used to discriminate against and persecute people. These events proved that a law may be legally valid but morally wrong. Therefore, people began to recognise the need for higher principles of justice and morality, which led to the revival of Natural Law.

2. Nuremberg Trials

After the Second World War, the Nuremberg Trials were held to prosecute major Nazi leaders for war crimes and crimes against humanity. The trials demonstrated that individuals could be held responsible for serious international crimes even when they claimed to have acted under the authority of their government. This strengthened the idea that State-made laws and government orders cannot always be treated as a complete justification for immoral acts. These developments gave renewed importance to the principles associated with Natural Law.

3. Growth of Human Rights

After the Second World War, the protection of human rights became an important international concern. The United Nations Charter of 1945 and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 emphasised human dignity, equality, liberty, and fundamental rights. These principles are closely connected with Natural Law, which recognises that certain basic rights belong to human beings by virtue of their humanity and should not depend entirely on the wishes of the State.

4. Weaknesses of Legal Positivism

Legal positivism, particularly the theory associated with John Austin, focuses on law as a system of rules recognised or established by legal authority. Critics argued that the separation of law and morality could not, by itself, provide a sufficient moral basis for opposing oppressive laws. The experience of Nazi Germany highlighted the danger of accepting a law merely because it had been enacted by the State. As a result, Natural Law regained importance as a way of examining whether laws are just and morally acceptable.

5. Need to Control Arbitrary State Power

The experience of dictatorship and political oppression showed that governments could misuse their legal powers against individuals and communities. Natural Law emphasises that State power should be guided by justice, reason, fairness, and respect for human dignity. It therefore contributed to the development of ideas associated with limited government, constitutionalism, and the protection of fundamental rights.

6. Role of Judges in the Interpretation of Law

Judges sometimes face situations in which the wording of a law is unclear or its application may lead to an unfair result. Principles such as justice, reasonableness, equality, and human dignity help courts interpret legal provisions within the limits of the applicable legal system. The need to protect individuals against arbitrary decisions strengthened interest in Natural Law and its moral principles.

7. Development of Constitutionalism and Judicial Review

Modern constitutional systems increasingly recognise that government power must operate within legal and constitutional limits. Written constitutions protect fundamental rights and authorise courts, under specified conditions, to review laws and government actions. These developments reflect concerns closely associated with Natural Law: the belief that the exercise of State power should respect justice, liberty, equality, and human dignity.

4. Features of Revived Natural Law

Revived Natural Law became important in the 20th century, especially after the Second World War. It emphasises the relationship between law, morality, justice, and human rights. Its main features are as follows:

1. Relationship Between Law and Morality

Revived Natural Law emphasises that law should not be separated completely from morality. Laws should be based on justice, fairness, and ethical principles.

2. Importance of Human Rights

It recognises the importance of basic human rights, such as life, liberty, equality, and human dignity. These rights should be protected against unjust State action.

3. Opposition to Unjust Laws

Revived Natural Law argues that a law may be legally valid but morally unjust. Therefore, laws should be judged not only by their legal authority but also by their justice and morality.

4. Limitation on State Power

It believes that the power of the State should not be unlimited. Governments must respect justice, human dignity, and the fundamental rights of individuals.

5. Support for Constitutionalism

Revived Natural Law supports the idea that government power should be limited by the Constitution and that fundamental rights should be protected by law.

6. Role of Judges

It recognises the importance of moral principles, fairness, and reasonableness in the interpretation of law. Judges may use these principles while interpreting legal provisions within the limits of the legal system.

7. Protection of Human Dignity

It emphasises that every human being deserves respect and fair treatment. Law should protect individuals from cruelty, discrimination, and injustice.

8. Promotion of the Common Good

Revived Natural Law considers human welfare and the common good important purposes of law. Law should promote a just, peaceful, and fair society.
