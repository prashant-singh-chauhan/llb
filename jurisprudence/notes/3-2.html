Module 03 · Analytical School

John Austin

Jurisprudence · Topic 3.1

Introduction

John Austin was the founder of the Analytical School of Jurisprudence and developed Imperative Theory of Law, also known as the Command Theory of Law.

He defined "law as the command of the sovereign backed by sanction."

According to Austin, every law can be understood as a command of the sovereign, which creates an obligation or duty on citizens and is supported by a sanction in case of disobedience.

He distinguished positive law from positive morality and emphasized the importance of sovereignty and obedience.

Austin also separated law from morality, making jurisprudence a scientific study of positive law. His ideas provided a systematic foundation for analytical jurisprudence and strongly influenced modern legal theory.

Meaning of Austin’s Statement

Austin's theory of law has three main elements: Command of the Sovereign, Obligation or Duty, and Sanction.

1. Command of the Sovereign

A command is an expression of the will or wish of a superior directing another person to do something or to abstain from doing something.

According to Austin, the command must come from the sovereign. The sovereign is a determinate person or body of persons who is habitually obeyed by the people and does not habitually obey any other earthly authority.

For example, when the State orders citizens to pay taxes according to law, it is a command of political authority.

Important sentence to remember: According to Austin, law is a command given by the sovereign to the people.

2. Obligation or Duty

A command creates a legal duty on the person to whom it is addressed.

If the law requires a person to pay tax, that person has a legal obligation to pay it. Thus, according to Austin, command and duty are closely connected.

Important sentence to remember: A command creates a duty, and the person receiving the command is legally required to obey it.

3. Sanction

A command is supported by a sanction, which means an adverse consequence or punishment for disobedience.

For example, if a person violates a criminal law, the law may provide imprisonment or a fine. The possibility of punishment gives force to the legal command.

Important sentence to remember: Sanction is the punishment or adverse consequence imposed for disobeying a legal command.

Conclusion

According to Austin, the three essential elements of law are command, duty, and sanction. The sovereign issues the command, the command creates a duty, and the sanction provides punishment for disobedience.

Easy formula to remember: Sovereign → Command → Duty → Sanction.

Criticism of Austin's Command Theory of Law

Although John Austin's Command Theory is an important contribution to Jurisprudence, it has several limitations. The main criticisms are as follows:

1. All Laws Are Not Commands

Not all laws command people to do or not to do something. Some laws confer legal powers on individuals and authorities. For example, laws relating to contracts, wills, and marriage provide conditions for exercising legal powers. If these conditions are not fulfilled, the transaction may be invalid, but punishment is not necessarily imposed. Therefore, all laws cannot be explained as commands backed by sanctions.

2. Constitutional Law

Austin's theory assumes that the sovereign has unlimited authority. However, in a modern constitutional democracy, the powers of the government are limited by the Constitution. The Constitution also distributes powers among different organs of the State. Therefore, the idea of an unlimited sovereign cannot adequately explain modern constitutional systems.

3. Customary Law

Many legal rules develop gradually from customs, traditions, and social practices. They may not originate from an express command of the sovereign. Austin explained recognised customs as commands approved by the sovereign, but this explanation does not fully describe how customary law develops and gains legal recognition.

4. International Law

International law governs relations between States, but there is no single world sovereign who commands all States. International law is based on treaties, international customs, and other recognised sources. Austin therefore classified it as positive morality rather than law in the strict sense. Critics argue that international law is still law even without a single world sovereign.

5. Habitual Obedience Is Insufficient

Austin defined the sovereign as a person or body that is habitually obeyed by the people. However, habitual obedience alone cannot fully explain the authority and continuity of law. When one ruler leaves office and another takes power, the legal system does not have to start again. Existing laws continue to operate, and the new authority derives its powers from the rules and institutions of the legal system.

6. Sanction Is Not the Only Basis of Obedience

Austin emphasised punishment or the threat of punishment as an important element of law. However, people obey laws for many reasons, including a sense of legal duty, acceptance of legal authority, and respect for social order. Therefore, fear of punishment alone cannot fully explain why people obey the law.

7. Role of Judges

Judges play an important role in interpreting legal provisions, applying precedents, and developing legal principles. In some cases, courts also clarify the law where existing rules are unclear or incomplete. Austin's command theory does not adequately explain the full creative and interpretative role of judges in the development of law.

8. Criticism by H.L.A. Hart

H.L.A. Hart strongly criticised Austin's theory. According to Hart, a legal system contains two types of rules:

1. Primary Rules: These rules impose duties on individuals, such as rules prohibiting theft.

2. Secondary Rules: These rules explain how laws are identified, created, changed, and applied by legal authorities.

Hart argued that Austin's command theory mainly explains rules that impose duties but fails to explain the full importance of rules that confer legal powers. Therefore, Hart considered Austin's theory too narrow to explain the complete nature of a modern legal system.
