Classical Natural Law

Jurisprudence · Topic 2.1

Introduction · Characteristics · Development · Importance · Application

1. Introduction

Natural Law is one of the oldest and most important schools of thought in Jurisprudence. It refers to a higher law based on nature, reason, morality, and justice, which is independent of laws made by the State.

Classical Natural Law developed through the ideas of Greek and Roman philosophers and medieval thinkers.

It teaches that human-made laws should be consistent with moral principles, justice, and fairness. According to natural law thinkers, a law that is unjust or contrary to morality lacks true moral authority. Thus, Natural Law provides a moral standard for judging the fairness and justice of laws made by the State.

2. Main characteristics of Natural Law
Feature Explanation
Universal It applies to all human beings, regardless of place or time.
Eternal Its basic principles are considered permanent and unchanging.
Based on Reason Human beings can understand right and wrong through reason.
Moral in Nature It is based on justice, fairness, and morality.
Superior to Human Law It provides a higher standard for judging laws made by the State.
Criticism of Unjust Laws A law contrary to natural justice and morality is considered unjust and lacks moral authority.
3. Historical Development of Classical Natural Law

Classical Natural Law developed gradually through the ideas of Greek philosophers, Roman thinkers, and medieval scholars. Its development is mainly studied through three periods: the Greek Period, the Roman Period, and the Medieval Period. Ancient Indian thought, particularly the ideas of Manu, also provides an important perspective on the relationship between law, morality, and justice.

The historical development of Classical Natural Law reflects the contributions of Greek philosophers, Roman thinkers, and medieval scholars. The Greeks established its philosophical foundation, the Romans developed its legal ideas, and medieval thinkers, especially St. Thomas Aquinas, connected it with divine law and morality. Manu's teachings on Dharma also provide a significant perspective from ancient Indian legal thought. These traditions highlight the importance of justice, morality, and proper conduct in understanding law.

A. Greek Period

The Greek philosophers laid the foundation of Classical Natural Law. They believed that the universe is governed by reason and that law should be based on justice.

1. Heraclitus

He believed that the universe is governed by a universal reason and follows a natural order.

2. Socrates

He emphasised morality and justice and believed that knowledge of right and wrong could be discovered through reason. According to him, there are certain moral principles which are not dependent upon the commands of rulers.

3. Plato

He believed that the main aim of the State is to establish justice. He supported the idea of an ideal society based on justice and moral principles.

4. Aristotle

He distinguished between natural justice and legal justice. Natural justice has a universal basis, whereas legal justice is based on rules made by human authorities.

5. Stoic Philosophers

They introduced the concept of Logos, meaning universal reason. They believed that all human beings share reason and are governed by a common natural law.

Thus, the Greek philosophers established the basic idea that law should follow nature, reason, and justice.

B. Roman Period

The Romans developed Greek ideas and applied them to their legal system.

1. Cicero

He believed that, "True law is right reason in agreement with nature." According to him, natural law is universal, eternal, and unchanging. Its fundamental principles cannot be changed merely by the decisions of the government or legislature.

2. Roman Jurists

Roman jurists developed important legal concepts, including:

Jus Civile: Law applicable to Roman citizens.

Jus Gentium: Law associated with dealings between Romans and foreigners.

Jus Naturale: Natural law, based on principles believed to arise from nature.

The Roman contribution was important because natural law became closely connected with the actual development of legal principles.

C. Medieval Period

During the medieval period, Natural Law became closely connected with religion and belief in God.

1. St. Augustine

He emphasised that law should be consistent with justice and the moral order. An unjust law lacks true moral authority.

2. St. Thomas Aquinas

He was the most important medieval thinker of Natural Law and gave a systematic theory of law. According to him, natural law is a part of eternal law of god, and human beings can understand it through reason.

Aquinas divided law into four types:

1. Eternal Law: God's plan governing the universe.

2. Divine Law: Law revealed by God through sacred teachings.

3. Natural Law: Human participation in eternal law through reason.

4. Human Law: Laws made by human authorities and the State.

The basic principle of natural law according to Aquinas is:

“Good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided.”

Human beings can understand basic principles of natural law through reason. Therefore, Aquinas did not treat human reason as irrelevant; rather, reason is the means by which human beings understand their participation in the divine order.

According to Aquinas, a human law which seriously conflicts with natural law loses its moral authority.

D. Ancient Indian Perspective: Manu

Manu is an important figure in ancient Indian legal thought. The Manusmriti, also known as the Manava-Dharmashastra, discusses Dharma, duties, justice, and the responsibilities of the king.

1. Concept of Dharma: Manu's teachings connected law with Dharma, which includes duty, proper conduct, and moral order.

2. Justice and Morality: Manu's legal thought emphasised that the administration of justice should follow Dharma and established rules of proper conduct.

3. Duties of the King: Manu stressed that the king should protect the people, administer justice, and punish wrongdoing according to Dharma.

Thus, Manu's ideas provide an important Indian perspective on the relationship between law, morality, duty, and justice. Although this tradition is distinct from the Western theory of Classical Natural Law, it shares concerns about the moral foundations of law.

4. Importance of Natural Law.

1. Basis of Justice

Natural Law provides a moral standard for judging whether a law is just or unjust. It says that law should not be accepted merely because it has been made by a proper authority. It should also satisfy basic principles of justice and fairness.

2. Protection of Human Rights

Natural Law played an important role in the development of the idea of natural and human rights. Rights such as life, liberty, and equality were considered rights belonging to human beings by nature and not merely gifts of the State.

3. Limitation on State Power

Natural Law places limits on the power of the State. Government cannot claim unlimited authority if its actions seriously violate basic principles of justice and human dignity. Thus, Natural Law supports the idea of limited government and the rule of law.

4. Development of Constitutionalism

The idea of a higher law influenced constitutionalism. Modern constitutions protect certain basic rights against arbitrary action by the government. In India, values such as justice, liberty, equality, and dignity are important parts of constitutional thinking. Natural Law ideas have also been associated with the interpretation and protection of Fundamental Rights.

5. Development of Natural Justice

Natural Law also influenced the principles of natural justice. These principles require fairness in legal and administrative decisions. For example, a person should generally be given a fair hearing before an adverse decision is made.

6. Help in Judicial Interpretation

Natural Law provides judges with broader principles such as justice, fairness, reasonableness, and human dignity while interpreting law. This becomes especially important when the written law is unclear or when different interpretations are possible.

7. Opposition to Unjust Laws

Natural Law provides a basis for criticising unjust laws. It says that there is a difference between law as it exists and law as it ought to be. Therefore, a law may be legally enacted but still be criticised as unjust or immoral.

8. Promotion of Human Welfare

The ultimate purpose of Natural Law is connected with human welfare and the common good. It encourages law to protect human beings and promote peaceful and just social life.

5. Application in the Indian Legal System
Introduction

Natural Law has an important influence on Indian law, although India follows a written Constitution and a system of positive law.
Natural Law developed from the ancient idea of universal justice and reason into a modern theory based on human rights, liberty, equality, and dignity.
It passed through Greek, Roman, medieval, and modern stages. In India, its influence can be seen in Fundamental Rights, natural justice, judicial review, constitutional values, and human dignity.
Thus, Natural Law has helped Indian law move beyond mere technical rules towards justice, fairness, and the protection of human rights.

1. Fundamental Rights

The Indian Constitution protects values such as equality, liberty, freedom, and personal dignity through Fundamental Rights. The Constitution is the supreme law of the land, and courts can invalidate laws or government actions that violate constitutional requirements.

2. Article 14 – Equality

Article 14 reflects the idea that law should be based on fairness and equality. Arbitrary State action can be challenged because equality before the law is an important principle of justice.

3. Article 21 – Life and Personal Liberty

The Supreme Court has given Article 21 a wide interpretation. The right to life does not mean merely physical existence; it also includes important aspects of a dignified life. This reflects the Natural Law idea that human dignity and liberty deserve protection.

4. Principles of Natural Justice

Natural justice is widely applied in Indian administrative and judicial law. Its two basic principles are:

1. Audi Alteram Partem: It means that the other side must be heard before a decision is made.

2. Nemo Judex in Causa Sua: It means that no one should be a judge in their own case.

These principles aim to ensure fairness and prevent injustice.

5. Judicial Review

Indian courts can examine legislative and executive actions and strike them down when they violate the Constitution. Judicial review provides protection against the arbitrary exercise of State power.

6. Directive Principles and Social Justice

The Constitution aims to promote social, economic, and political justice. These constitutional goals reflect the Natural Law idea that law should ultimately serve justice and human welfare.
