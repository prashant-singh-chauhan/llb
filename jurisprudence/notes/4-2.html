Module 04 · Historical School

Frederick Karl Von Savigny

Jurisprudence · Topic 4.2

Introduction · Meaning · Features · Critical Analysis · Conclusion

1. Introduction

The Volksgeist Theory was propounded by Friedrich Carl von Savigny, a prominent German jurist and the principal representative of the Historical School of Jurisprudence.

The term Volksgeist is derived from the German language and means the spirit of the people or the common consciousness of a nation. According to Savigny, law is not merely created by the State or a legislative authority. Instead, it develops gradually from the customs, traditions, beliefs, and common experiences of the people.

Savigny believed that law is closely connected with the historical life of a nation. Just as language and culture develop naturally within a community, law also grows through the shared consciousness and social practices of its people.

Therefore, the central idea of the Volksgeist Theory is that law develops organically from the common consciousness of the people and reflects the historical character of the society to which it belongs.

2. Meaning of Volksgeist Theory

Volksgeist Theory explains the origin and development of law through the collective consciousness, customs, traditions, and historical experiences of a community.

According to Savigny, law is not an arbitrary creation of a ruler or legislator. It emerges gradually from the common life of the people and develops as society progresses through different historical stages.

In the early stages of society, law is primarily expressed through customs and popular practices. As society becomes more complex, legal scholars and jurists help identify, interpret, and systematically develop these existing legal principles.

Thus, law reflects the historical identity of a nation rather than merely the personal wishes of an individual legislator.

Example: A community may follow certain customs relating to marriage, inheritance, or property for generations. These practices may influence the development of customary law when they satisfy the applicable requirements for legal recognition.

3. Salient Features of Volksgeist Theory
1. Law Originates from the Spirit of the People

The main feature of the theory is that law originates from the common consciousness of the people. It reflects their shared traditions, beliefs, experiences, and social practices.

According to Savigny, law cannot be fully understood without understanding the historical and cultural life of the community in which it develops.

2. Law Is Not Merely the Creation of the State

Savigny rejected the view that all law originates exclusively from the commands of the sovereign or the decisions of the legislature.

He argued that law often develops through social customs and historical practices before it is formally recognised or organised by legal authorities.

This does not mean that legislation has no role in lawmaking. Rather, Savigny's theory emphasises that legislation should take account of the historical and social foundations of a legal system.

3. Importance of Custom

Custom occupies an important place in the Volksgeist Theory because it expresses the common consciousness of the people.

Customs develop through repeated social practices and may reflect the established expectations and values of a community. Over time, certain customs may become recognised as legally binding rules.

Therefore, custom is an important means through which the spirit of the people finds expression in law.

4. Organic Growth of Law

According to Savigny, law grows organically with society. It develops gradually through the experiences, customs, and changing circumstances of a nation.

Law is therefore comparable to language, which develops through the life of a community rather than being invented completely by a single individual.

This principle explains why legal systems should be studied in their historical context.

5. Law and Historical Development

The theory maintains that law is closely connected with the history of a nation. Legal rules and institutions develop through different historical periods and reflect the circumstances of the society in which they emerge.

Consequently, the laws of different nations may differ because their histories, customs, and social experiences are different.

6. Role of Jurists

Savigny recognised the importance of jurists in the development of law. As society becomes more complex, legal scholars help identify, interpret, organise, and systematically develop legal principles that have emerged historically.

Thus, jurists do not simply invent law independently of society. They contribute to the systematic development of legal principles rooted in the historical experience of the community.

7. Opposition to Arbitrary Legislation

Savigny opposed the idea of introducing a complete legal code without adequately considering the history, customs, and social conditions of the nation.

He believed that legal reforms should be informed by the actual development of society. Laws imposed without regard to a nation's historical circumstances may fail to reflect its social needs.

However, the theory does not necessarily reject all legislation. It stresses the importance of historical understanding when creating or reforming laws.

8. Law Reflects National Character

According to the Volksgeist Theory, the legal system of a nation reflects its historical identity, customs, and collective experiences.

Since different societies have different historical backgrounds, their legal systems may develop in different ways. A legal system should therefore be understood in relation to the society from which it emerged.

9. Relationship Between Law and Society

The theory emphasises the close relationship between law and the social life of a community. Law develops alongside social institutions, relationships, and established practices.

Changes in society may gradually influence legal development. Therefore, law should not be studied as an isolated set of rules independent of the people and institutions it governs.

10. Importance of Legal Continuity

The Volksgeist Theory gives importance to continuity in the development of law. Existing legal institutions often reflect long periods of social experience and historical development.

Understanding this continuity helps explain why legal rules persist and how established principles change over time.

Nevertheless, historical continuity should not prevent necessary reforms, especially where existing customs conflict with fundamental rights, equality, or constitutional principles.

4. Criticism of Henry Sumner Maine's Theory

Henry Sumner Maine made a significant contribution to the Historical School of Jurisprudence by explaining the development of law through historical and comparative study. His theory that societies generally move from status to contract is influential, but it has also been criticised for being too broad and for not fully explaining the complexity of legal and social development.

1. Overgeneralisation of Legal Development

Maine's famous proposition that the movement of progressive societies has been from status to contract may be an oversimplification. Different societies have followed different historical paths, and legal development has not occurred uniformly. Some societies have retained strong status-based institutions even while developing contractual relationships.

2. Status Has Not Disappeared in Modern Society

Maine's theory may suggest that contract gradually replaces status. However, status continues to influence modern legal systems. Family relationships, citizenship, age, inheritance, employment protections and other legal relationships may create rights and duties independently of an individual's freely negotiated agreement. Therefore, status and contract coexist rather than one completely replacing the other.

3. Limited Applicability to All Societies

Maine's theory was largely developed through his study of ancient Roman law, Hindu law and other historical legal institutions. Critics argue that conclusions drawn from these examples cannot automatically be applied to every society. Different cultures and legal traditions have developed according to their own social, political, economic and historical conditions.

4. Excessive Emphasis on the Family and Patriarchal Authority

Maine gave considerable importance to the patriarchal family and the authority of the male head of the household in early legal systems. This approach may not adequately explain the diversity of family structures and social organisation across different societies. It also risks presenting one historical pattern as more universal than the evidence supports.

5. Insufficient Attention to Economic and Political Factors

Maine emphasised historical institutions, customs and the evolution of legal relationships. However, legal change may also be driven by economic interests, political struggles, class relations, technological developments and organised social movements. His status-to-contract formula does not, by itself, fully explain the influence of these forces on legal development.

6. Contract Does Not Always Represent Complete Freedom

Maine associated the movement towards contract with greater individual choice and independence. In practice, however, contractual relationships may involve unequal bargaining power. A worker, tenant or consumer may formally agree to contractual terms without having a genuinely equal opportunity to negotiate them. Thus, the growth of contract does not necessarily guarantee substantive freedom or equality.

7. The Theory Is Not a Universal or Inevitable Law of Progress

The movement from status to contract should be understood as a broad historical observation rather than an inevitable rule governing every society. Modern legal systems sometimes introduce new status-based protections, such as mandatory employment rights and consumer safeguards, to correct inequalities arising from contractual relationships. Legal development may therefore involve movement in both directions.

8. Limited Explanation of Legislation and Deliberate Legal Reform

Maine's historical approach concentrated on understanding law through its development over time. Critics may argue that this approach gives insufficient attention to the role of deliberate legislation, constitutional reform and judicial decisions in changing existing institutions. Historical development is important, but law can also be transformed through conscious legal and political action.

9. The Theory Does Not Fully Explain the Relationship Between Status and Contract

Status and contract are not always separate or opposing categories. A person's legal status may determine the capacity to enter into a contract, while contractual relationships may create or modify certain legal rights and obligations. Consequently, the two concepts often interact rather than operate as successive and mutually exclusive stages of legal development.

Despite these criticisms, Maine's theory remains an important contribution to historical jurisprudence. It highlights the relationship between social organisation and legal institutions and helps explain the growing importance of individual choice in modern legal systems. Its value lies in offering a useful framework for understanding certain historical changes, rather than a universal explanation of the development of every legal system.

5. Short Note for Examination

The Volksgeist Theory was propounded by Friedrich Carl von Savigny. Volksgeist means the spirit of the people. According to Savigny, law develops organically from the common consciousness, customs, traditions, and historical experiences of a nation. It is not merely the creation of the State or legislature.

The main features of the theory are the organic growth of law, the importance of custom, the influence of historical development, the role of jurists, and the relationship between law and society. Its main criticism is that excessive emphasis on tradition may obstruct necessary legal reforms and preserve unjust customs.

Principal Doctrines of Savigny’s Thesis of Law
  • Law is a product of the people’s consciousness, known as Volksgeist or spirit of the people.
  • Law develops gradually through customs and traditions.
  • Law is closely connected with the history and culture of society.
  • Law should not be artificially created without considering social conditions.
  • Law develops from popular customs and is later shaped by legal scholars.
