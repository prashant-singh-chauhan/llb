Other Important 4 Marks Questions

1. Manu

Manu was an ancient Indian lawgiver whose teachings are mainly contained in the Manusmriti, also known as the Code of Manu. He discussed Dharma, duties, social conduct, marriage, property, punishment, and administration of justice. Manu considered Dharma the foundation of law and social order. He emphasized the king's duty to maintain justice and protect society. His teachings made a significant contribution to ancient Indian legal thought and jurisprudence, influencing Hindu law and social institutions.

2. Importance of Jurisprudence

Jurisprudence is important because it provides a systematic and scientific study of law. It helps us understand the nature, meaning, purpose, and principles of law. It develops legal reasoning and analytical thinking among lawyers and judges. Jurisprudence also helps in understanding the relationship between law, society, morality, justice, and the State. It assists in interpreting legal rules, removing ambiguities, and developing new legal principles. Thus, jurisprudence forms the theoretical foundation of legal education and practice.

3. Is Jurisprudence the Study of the Law of One Nation?

No, jurisprudence is not limited to the laws of one particular nation. It is the systematic study of the fundamental principles, nature, and purpose of law. It examines legal concepts such as rights, duties, justice, liability, possession, ownership, and sovereignty. John Salmond defined jurisprudence as the science of the first principles of civil law. Jurisprudence studies the underlying principles of law rather than merely the specific statutes of a single country. However, some branches of jurisprudence focus on particular legal systems.

4. Historical Jurisprudence

Historical Jurisprudence is a branch of jurisprudence that studies the origin, development, and evolution of law through history. It considers law as a product of the customs, traditions, beliefs, and social practices of people. Friedrich Carl von Savigny is its chief representative. He propounded the doctrine of Volksgeist, meaning the spirit of the people. According to this school, law develops gradually and organically with society rather than being created suddenly by legislation or imposed by rulers.

5. General Jurisprudence

General Jurisprudence means the study of the general principles of law that are common to different legal systems. It deals with concepts such as rights, duties, possession, ownership, and liability. Its purpose is to understand the basic principles of law without restricting the study to one particular country.

6. Particular Jurisprudence

Particular Jurisprudence means the study of the law of a particular country or legal system. For example, the study of the fundamental principles of Indian law may be considered particular jurisprudence. Thus, general jurisprudence examines principles across legal systems, while particular jurisprudence focuses on a specific legal system.

7. Ground Norm

Ground Norm (Grundnorm) is the central concept of Hans Kelsen's Pure Theory of Law. According to Kelsen, every legal rule derives its validity from a higher legal rule. At the top of this hierarchy is the Grundnorm, or basic norm, which is presupposed as valid. It provides the ultimate foundation for explaining the validity of the entire legal system. Thus, the Ground Norm occupies the highest position in Kelsen's hierarchy of legal norms.

8. Sanction

Sanction means the punishment or legal consequence imposed for violating a law. According to John Austin, law is a command of the sovereign backed by a sanction. Sanctions encourage obedience by creating consequences for the violation of legal rules. They may include imprisonment, fines, penalties, or other legal consequences. Thus, sanctions are an important element of Austin's Command Theory and help enforce legal rules and maintain social order.

9. Contribution of Jeremy Bentham

Jeremy Bentham was the founder of Utilitarian Jurisprudence. He considered the principle of “the greatest happiness of the greatest number” fundamental to law and morality. He criticised unclear and outdated laws and supported the codification of laws to make them clear, certain, and accessible. Bentham distinguished between expository jurisprudence, which explains existing law, and censorial jurisprudence, which examines what the law ought to be. His utilitarian approach influenced legal reform and modern legislation.

10. Contribution of John Austin

John Austin was the leading representative of the Analytical School of Jurisprudence and developed the Command Theory of Law. He defined law as the command of the sovereign backed by sanction. He distinguished positive law from positive morality and emphasised sovereignty and obedience. Austin also advocated studying positive law separately from questions of morality. His ideas provided a systematic foundation for analytical jurisprudence and strongly influenced modern legal theory.

11. Is International Law Law According to Austin?

According to John Austin, international law is not law in the strict sense of his Command Theory. Austin defined law as a command of a sovereign backed by sanction. International law is not made by a single supreme sovereign with general authority over all States. Therefore, Austin classified it as positive morality rather than positive law. However, modern jurists disagree with this view because international law contains recognised rules, institutions, and enforcement mechanisms that regulate relations between States.

12. Contribution of Henry Maine

Sir Henry Maine was a leading jurist of the Historical School of Jurisprudence. He studied the historical development of legal institutions by comparing different societies. His famous proposition was that the movement of progressive societies has been “from Status to Contract”. Maine emphasised that law develops through customs, traditions, and social practices. He also studied ancient legal systems and patriarchal institutions. His comparative and historical method greatly contributed to understanding the evolution of law and society.

13. Contribution of H. L. A. Hart

H. L. A. Hart was an important modern legal positivist. In his famous work The Concept of Law, he criticised Austin's Command Theory and explained law as a union of primary and secondary rules. Primary rules impose duties, while secondary rules provide methods for creating, changing, and identifying laws. Hart introduced the important concept of the Rule of Recognition, which helps determine the validity of legal rules within a legal system. His theory provided a modern and systematic explanation of legal systems and their functioning.

14. Contribution of Savigny

Friedrich Carl von Savigny was the chief representative of the Historical School of Jurisprudence. He developed the concept of Volksgeist, meaning the spirit of the people. According to Savigny, law develops naturally from the customs, traditions, beliefs, and common consciousness of society. He opposed the immediate codification of law when society was not ready for it. His historical approach established that law is closely connected with the history, culture, and social development of people.

15. Contribution of Justice Holmes

Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. was an important thinker associated with American Legal Realism. He viewed law from a practical perspective rather than treating it merely as a system of logical rules. Holmes famously stated that “the life of the law has not been logic; it has been experience.” He emphasised that experience, social needs, and practical consequences are important in understanding law. His ideas contributed significantly to legal realism and encouraged lawyers and judges to study the actual working of law in society.

16. Contribution of Salmond

John William Salmond was an important jurist who contributed greatly to the systematic study of jurisprudence. He defined jurisprudence as the “science of the first principles of the civil law.” His contributions include the classification of jurisprudence into general and particular jurisprudence and the systematic analysis of rights, duties, possession, ownership, property, and liability. He also explained law in relation to courts and the administration of justice. His systematic approach greatly influenced modern legal education and jurisprudential study.

17. Principal Doctrines of Savigny's Theory of Law

1. Volksgeist: Law is a product of the people's common consciousness, known as Volksgeist or the spirit of the people.

2. Gradual Development: Law develops gradually through the customs and traditions of society.

3. Connection with History: Law is closely connected with the history and culture of society.

4. Opposition to Artificial Lawmaking: Law should not be created artificially without considering the social conditions and traditions of the people.

5. Role of Legal Scholars: Law develops from popular customs and is later analysed, refined, and systematised by legal scholars and jurists.

18. Contribution of Leon Duguit

Léon Duguit was a prominent jurist of the Sociological School of Jurisprudence. His main contribution was the Doctrine of Social Solidarity, according to which individuals are interdependent and law should promote social cooperation. He rejected the idea of absolute individual rights and emphasised social duties and responsibilities. Duguit believed that the State and individuals should work for the welfare of society. He considered social solidarity a foundation for understanding the purpose and operation of law in society.

19. Social Solidarity

Social Solidarity is an important concept in the Sociological School of Jurisprudence, mainly associated with Léon Duguit. It means the interdependence and cooperation among members of society. Duguit believed that individuals cannot live independently because they depend on one another to satisfy their needs. Therefore, law should recognise social interdependence and promote social cooperation and public welfare. His theory gives particular importance to social duties and collective welfare while recognising that legal systems must also address individual interests.

20. Jurisprudence Is the Lawyer's Extraversion: Discuss

Julius Stone described jurisprudence as “the lawyer's extraversion.” By this, he meant that jurisprudence takes a lawyer beyond the narrow boundaries of legal rules and encourages the study of law in relation to society, history, economics, politics, philosophy, and morality. It broadens the lawyer's outlook and develops a deeper understanding of legal problems. Thus, jurisprudence enables lawyers to critically examine legal principles, their purposes, and their social consequences rather than merely applying existing rules.

21. Contribution of Aristotle

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) was a Greek philosopher who made an important contribution to the study of law and justice. He divided justice into distributive justice and corrective justice. Distributive justice concerns the fair distribution of benefits and burdens, while corrective justice deals with correcting wrongs between individuals. He also distinguished between natural justice and legal justice. Aristotle believed that law should promote justice, fairness, and the common good of society.

22. Interest

In jurisprudence, an interest means a claim, demand, or expectation of a person that is recognised and protected by law. According to Rudolf von Jhering, law exists to protect and balance various human interests in society. Interests may be classified as individual, public, or social. The purpose of law is to protect legitimate interests and prevent their violation. Thus, the concept of interest explains the relationship between law, rights, duties, and social needs.

23. Pound's Classification of Legally Protected Interests

Roscoe Pound, a leading jurist of the Sociological School of Jurisprudence, classified legally protected interests into three main categories.

1. Individual Interests: These concern the personality, family relationships, property, and other interests of individuals.

2. Public Interests: These concern the interests of the State as a legal entity, including its security and proper functioning.

3. Social Interests: These concern the welfare and security of society, including public health, public safety, social institutions, and general progress.

Pound believed that law should balance these competing interests to achieve social order and justice.

24. Theory of Natural Law

The Theory of Natural Law states that law is connected with nature, reason, morality, and justice, rather than consisting only of rules made by the State. It considers certain principles of right and wrong to have a general or universal significance. Thinkers such as Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas, and John Locke contributed to the development of natural law thought. Thomas Aquinas argued that an unjust law may lack the moral authority of a true law. Natural law therefore connects the study of law with morality and justice.

25. Law Is Not All Morality: Discuss

The statement “Law is not all morality” means that law and morality are related but not identical. Morality consists of principles of right and wrong, while law consists of rules recognised and enforced by legal institutions. Many immoral acts are not necessarily illegal, and every legal act may not be morally right. John Austin distinguished positive law from morality and argued that the legal validity of a rule depends on its source rather than simply on its moral value.

26. Relation Between Jurisprudence and Political Science

Jurisprudence and Political Science are closely interrelated. Political Science studies the State, government, political authority, and the institutions through which public power is exercised. Jurisprudence studies the nature, principles, and concepts of law, including the laws that establish and regulate State institutions. Political decisions often influence legislation, while constitutional and other legal rules regulate government power. Therefore, understanding a legal system requires consideration of its political structure, and understanding the State also requires knowledge of its legal framework.

27. What Do You Understand by Analytical Positivism?

Analytical Positivism is a school of legal philosophy that studies law as it exists, rather than judging its validity solely by morality or justice. It emphasises the study of positive law, meaning law laid down or recognised by legal authorities. John Austin viewed law as the command of the sovereign backed by sanctions. Later, H. L. A. Hart developed legal positivism further by explaining law through the union of primary and secondary rules. Analytical positivism aims to provide a systematic understanding of the nature and structure of law.

28. Factors and Circumstances That Led to the Economic Approach to Law

1. Industrialisation and Urbanisation: Industrial growth and the expansion of cities created complex economic and social problems that required legal solutions.

2. Growth of Trade and Markets: Increasing business activities created a need for laws regulating contracts, property, competition, and commercial relationships.

3. Efficient Use of Resources: The need to allocate limited resources encouraged scholars to examine the economic effects of legal rules.

4. Social Welfare: The growing importance of public welfare encouraged the study of how law affects social costs, benefits, and economic opportunities.

5. Contribution of Thinkers: Thinkers such as Jeremy Bentham, Ronald Coase, and Richard Posner contributed to the development of economic analysis of law in different historical periods.

Thus, the economic approach studies law in relation to economic efficiency, incentives, resource allocation, and social welfare.

29. Law Is the Vanishing Point of Jurisprudence: Discuss

The statement “Law is the vanishing point of jurisprudence” is commonly associated with John Austin. It expresses the idea that law is the central subject around which jurisprudence develops. Jurisprudence studies the fundamental principles, concepts, nature, and purpose of law. It does not deal merely with individual legal rules but examines their underlying principles. Therefore, discussions about rights, duties, legal validity, justice, and legal institutions ultimately relate to the understanding of law. In this sense, law is the ultimate focus of jurisprudence.

30. “Certainty of Law Is a Legal Myth”: Discuss in Brief

The statement “Certainty of law is a legal myth” is associated with Jerome Frank, a prominent thinker of American Legal Realism. He argued that law cannot provide complete certainty because judicial decisions may depend on disputed facts, evidence, and judicial interpretation. Legal rules may also contain ambiguities and require interpretation in changing circumstances. Therefore, although law aims to provide certainty and predictability, absolute certainty is difficult to achieve. Legal certainty is an important goal, but the practical operation of law can involve uncertainty.

31. Stammler's Theory of Natural Law

Rudolf Stammler viewed law as a means of achieving justice and a harmonious social order. He believed that legal principles should be evaluated in the light of justice and changing social needs. He distinguished between the form and content of law: its form provides a framework, while its content changes according to social conditions. His theory is known as Natural Law with Variable Content. It maintains that the idea of just law must be applied to changing circumstances rather than treated as a fixed set of rules for all times.

32. Kohler's Theory of Natural Law

Joseph Kohler explained law in relation to the civilisation and culture of society. He rejected the idea that legal principles could be fully understood without considering the changing conditions, needs, and ideals of the people. According to Kohler, law develops with cultural progress and should contribute to the advancement of civilisation. His approach emphasised the relationship between legal rules and the cultural development of society and is often described as a cultural approach to law.

33. What Was Karl Marx's Approach to Law? Write in Brief.

According to Karl Marx, law is closely related to the economic structure of society. The economic system influences the social, political, and legal institutions of society. Marxist theory argues that law in a class-divided society may help the economically dominant class protect its property and interests. Thus, law can reflect class struggle and class domination. Marx envisaged that the development of a classless society would remove class-based exploitation. In that future society, the coercive role of the State and law was expected to decline as class divisions disappeared.

Other Important 10 Marks Questions

1. Law and Morals

Law and morality are two important systems that regulate human conduct. Law consists of rules enforced by the State, whereas morality consists of principles of right and wrong based on social, ethical and individual values.

Relationship between Law and Morals

Law and morality are closely related because many laws are based on moral principles. Rules against murder, theft, cheating and violence reflect basic moral values of society. Morality can also influence the development and reform of law.

However, law and morality are not identical. A legal rule is generally enforceable by the State, while a moral rule is usually enforced through social opinion, conscience or personal beliefs. For example, failing to help a stranger may be considered morally wrong but is not necessarily a legal offence.

John Austin, a positivist jurist, maintained that the existence of law is separate from its moral merit. According to him, a law does not cease to be law merely because it is unjust.

H. L. A. Hart also distinguished law from morality while recognising that there may be important connections between them. He accepted that morality can influence the development and interpretation of law.

Conclusion

Thus, law and morals are distinct but closely connected. Law provides externally enforceable rules, while morality provides standards of ethical conduct. A good legal system generally seeks to maintain justice and reflect fundamental moral values.

2. Difference Between Austin and Kelsen as Jurists

John Austin and Hans Kelsen were important jurists associated with Analytical Jurisprudence. Both studied positive law, but they explained its nature and validity differently.

1. Theory of Law

Austin propounded the Command Theory of Law, whereas Kelsen developed the Pure Theory of Law.

2. Meaning of Law

According to Austin, law is the command of the sovereign backed by sanction. Kelsen considered law a system of legal norms that prescribe what ought to be done.

3. Source of Validity

For Austin, law derives its authority from the sovereign. According to Kelsen, each legal norm gets its validity from a higher norm, ultimately based on the Grundnorm or Basic Norm.

4. Concept of Sovereignty

Austin considered the sovereign the supreme political authority in a society. Kelsen did not make a personal sovereign the foundation of legal validity; he focused on the legal order and its hierarchy of norms.

5. Role of Sanction

Austin treated sanction as an essential element of his command theory. Kelsen also regarded sanctions as important to law, but explained them through the structure of legal norms.

6. Hierarchy and Continuity of Law

Austin did not develop a hierarchy of legal norms. Kelsen explained law as a hierarchy of norms, which helps explain the continuity and validity of a legal system.

Conclusion

Thus, Austin explained law mainly through sovereign commands and sanctions, while Kelsen explained it through a hierarchical system of legal norms. Both contributed significantly to the development of analytical jurisprudence.

3. Jurisprudence is the eye of law. Discuss

Jurisprudence is the systematic study of the fundamental principles, concepts and theories of law. The statement “Jurisprudence is the eye of law” highlights its importance in understanding the nature, purpose and proper application of law.

Just as the eye helps a person see things clearly, jurisprudence helps lawyers, judges and students understand the deeper meaning of legal rules. It does not merely explain what the law is; it also examines why law exists, what purpose it serves and how it relates to justice.

Jurisprudence deals with important legal concepts such as rights, duties, liability, possession, ownership, justice and punishment. It also studies the relationship between law, morality, society and the State. This knowledge helps legal professionals interpret legal rules more clearly and understand the principles behind judicial decisions.

Jurisprudence also assists in law-making and legal reform. When society changes, its principles help lawmakers and courts examine whether existing laws remain suitable and fair. It encourages critical thinking and helps identify weaknesses in legal rules.

Conclusion

Thus, jurisprudence is rightly described as the “eye of law” because it provides a deeper understanding of the nature, purpose and principles of law. It guides legal thinking and contributes to the proper interpretation, development and administration of justice.

4. “Law grows with growth, strengthens with the strength of people and finally dies away as a stranger lost in its nationality.” — Discuss
Introduction

This statement is related to Friedrich Carl von Savigny, the chief representative of the Historical School of Jurisprudence. According to Savigny, law does not develop suddenly according to the wishes of rulers. It grows gradually from the customs, traditions, beliefs and common consciousness of the people.

Meaning of the Statement

Savigny introduced the concept of Volksgeist, which means the spirit of the people. He believed that law is closely connected with the history, culture and social life of a nation, just as language develops naturally among its people.

The statement means that law develops with society. As people's needs and social conditions change, their laws also develop. Law becomes stronger when it reflects the people's traditions and is accepted by society. If a nation gradually loses its distinctive culture and historical identity, its traditional legal system may also lose its original character.

Savigny's View on Legislation

Savigny opposed the premature codification of law without proper understanding of a nation's history and customs. However, he did not reject every form of legislation. He believed that legal scholars and lawmakers should respect the historical development of law and the needs of society.

Conclusion

Thus, Savigny considered law a product of the history, customs and collective consciousness of the people. His theory explains that law should develop naturally with society and remain connected with its cultural and historical roots.

5. “The Basis of Natural Law Was Man's Reason and Wisdom and Not the Spiritual Power of God” — Discuss
Introduction

Natural Law refers to principles of justice and right conduct that are considered to arise from human nature and reason. In its early development, Natural Law was closely connected with religion and the authority of God. Later, some thinkers gave greater importance to human reason and wisdom as the basis for understanding natural law.

Religious Approach to Natural Law

St. Thomas Aquinas explained that Natural Law is a part of God's Eternal Law. According to him, human beings can understand its principles through their reason and intellect. Therefore, Aquinas connected Natural Law with both divine authority and human reason.

Rationalist Approach to Natural Law

A major change came with the rationalist approach, especially associated with Hugo Grotius. He considered Natural Law a dictate of right reason. According to him, human beings can discover principles of right and wrong by understanding human nature and the need for peaceful social life. Thus, Natural Law could be understood through reason without depending entirely on religious authority.

Conclusion

Thus, the statement mainly represents the rationalist approach to Natural Law, which emphasises human reason and wisdom. However, it does not apply to every Natural Law theory. While Aquinas connected Natural Law with God, Grotius gave greater importance to reason, human nature and social relationships.
