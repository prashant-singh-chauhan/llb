1. When Does a Right of Action Come to an End in Tort Law?

Introduction:

A right of action means the legal right of an injured person to bring a case against another person for a wrongful act. In tort law, this right may come to an end under certain circumstances.

1. Death of the Parties

• The death of the plaintiff or defendant may bring a personal action to an end.

• It is based on the maxim Actio Personalis Moritur Cum Persona.

• However, certain legal claims survive the death of the parties.

2. Expiry of Limitation Period

• Every legal action must be filed within the time prescribed by law.

• If a person fails to file a case within the limitation period, the remedy may become time-barred.

3. Waiver

• Waiver means voluntarily giving up a legal right.

• If a person knowingly gives up their right to claim compensation, they may lose the right to bring an action.

4. Accord and Satisfaction

• It means settlement of a dispute between the parties.

• When the injured person accepts agreed compensation in full settlement of the claim, the right to further action generally comes to an end.

5. Release

• Release means voluntarily freeing another person from legal liability.

• Once a valid release is given, the injured person generally cannot claim compensation again for the same matter.

6. Judgment of the Court (Res Judicata)

• When a competent court finally decides a dispute, the same matter cannot generally be brought again between the same parties.

• This principle is known as Res Judicata.

Conclusion:

Thus, a right of action in tort law may come to an end due to death, expiry of limitation, waiver, accord and satisfaction, release and final judgment of the court. These principles prevent unnecessary litigation and provide certainty in legal matters.

2. Defences of Tort Liability (General Defences in the Law of Torts)

Introduction:

In the law of torts, a person who commits a wrongful act and causes injury to another person is generally held liable. However, there are certain circumstances in which a person can avoid legal liability. These are known as General Defences of Tort Liability. They are recognised by law and depend upon the facts and circumstances of each case.

1. Volenti Non Fit Injuria (Consent)

• This Latin maxim means that no injury is done to a person who willingly accepts the risk.

• If a person voluntarily agrees to face a particular risk, they generally cannot claim compensation for the resulting injury.

Example: A person participates in a boxing match and suffers an injury during the ordinary course of the game.

Case Law: Hall v. Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1933).

2. Plaintiff is the Wrongdoer

• A person may be denied compensation if their injury is directly connected with their own illegal act.

• A person cannot generally take advantage of their own wrongful conduct.

Example: A thief enters another person's property illegally and gets injured while committing theft.

3. Inevitable Accident

• An inevitable accident is an accident that could not have been avoided even after taking reasonable care and precautions.

• In such cases, the defendant is generally not held liable because the accident was neither intentional nor caused by negligence.

Example: An unexpected accident occurs despite the driver taking reasonable care.

Case Law: Stanley v. Powell (1891).

4. Act of God

• An Act of God means an extraordinary natural event that cannot be prevented by human care or foresight.

• It may include earthquakes, floods, lightning and extraordinary storms.

Example: A sudden and extraordinary flood destroys a person's property.

Case Law: Nichols v. Marsland (1876).

5. Private Defence

• Every person has the right to protect their own body and property against unlawful attacks.

• A person may use reasonable force to protect themselves or their property.

• However, the force used must not be excessive.

Example: A person pushes away an attacker to protect themselves from physical injury.

6. Necessity

• Necessity means doing an otherwise wrongful act to prevent greater harm.

• A person may avoid liability if the act was reasonably necessary to protect life or property.

Example: Breaking the door of a house to rescue a person trapped inside during a fire.

7. Statutory Authority

• When an act is authorised by law or a statute, a person may avoid liability for harm caused by performing that authorised act.

• The person must act within the authority granted by law.

Example: A government authority carries out legally authorised road construction work that causes unavoidable inconvenience to nearby residents.

8. Mistake

• Mistake means an incorrect belief about a fact or law.

• Generally, mistake of fact or law is not a defence in tort.

• A person cannot ordinarily escape liability merely by claiming that they were unaware of the facts or law.

Conclusion:

General defences play an important role in tort law because they protect persons from unfair liability in appropriate circumstances. The important defences are consent, plaintiff being the wrongdoer, inevitable accident, Act of God, private defence, necessity, statutory authority and mistake. Their application depends upon the facts and circumstances of each case.

3. Head Remedies in Tort Law (Remedies for Tortious Liability)

Introduction:

In the law of torts, a remedy means the legal relief given to an injured person whose legal right has been violated by another person. The main purpose of remedies is to compensate the injured person and protect their legal rights.

1. Judicial Remedies

Judicial remedies are remedies provided by the court. They are mainly divided into three types:

(a) Damages:

• Damages mean monetary compensation given by the court to the injured person.

• The purpose is to compensate the victim for the loss or injury suffered.

• Types of damages include:

1. Nominal Damages – A small amount awarded when a legal right is violated but no actual loss is proved.

2. Contemptuous Damages – A very small amount awarded when the court considers the claim technically valid but does not approve of the plaintiff's conduct.

3. Compensatory Damages – Money awarded to compensate for actual loss or injury.

4. Exemplary or Punitive Damages – Damages awarded in exceptional cases to punish wrongful conduct and discourage similar acts.

(b) Injunction:

• An injunction is an order of the court directing a person to do or stop doing a particular act.

• It is generally used to prevent continuing or future harm.

• Types of injunctions include:

1. Temporary Injunction – Granted for a limited period or until further orders of the court.

2. Permanent Injunction – A final order restraining a person from doing a particular act.

Example: A court orders a factory to stop releasing harmful smoke into a residential area.

(c) Specific Restitution of Property:

• It means restoring the property to its rightful owner.

• The court may order the return of property wrongfully taken or detained by another person.

Example: A person is ordered by the court to return a vehicle wrongfully taken from its owner.

2. Extra-Judicial Remedies

Extra-judicial remedies are remedies available to a person without first approaching the court. These remedies must be exercised within the limits of law.

(a) Self-Defence:

• A person can protect their own body and property against unlawful attacks.

• Only reasonable and necessary force should be used.

(b) Expulsion of Trespasser:

• A person who is in lawful possession of property may remove a trespasser using reasonable force.

• The force used must be proportionate and lawful.

(c) Re-entry on Land:

• A person who has been wrongfully dispossessed of their land may regain possession by lawful and peaceful means.

• Force should not be used beyond what the law permits.

(d) Abatement of Nuisance:

• A person may remove or stop a nuisance affecting their property by taking reasonable steps.

• The action must be lawful and should not cause unnecessary damage.

Example: Removing branches that unlawfully extend over one's property, subject to applicable legal limits.

Conclusion:

Remedies in tort law protect the legal rights of individuals and provide relief against wrongful acts. The main remedies are damages, injunction and specific restitution of property. Extra-judicial remedies also allow individuals to protect their rights within the limits of law. Thus, remedies play an important role in maintaining justice and preventing wrongful conduct.

4. Conditions of Liability for Foreign Tort

Introduction:

A foreign tort means a wrongful act committed in a country other than the country where the case is filed. For example, if a person commits a wrongful act in France and the injured person files a case in England, it is a case of foreign tort. The liability for a foreign tort depends upon certain legal conditions. These conditions help the court decide whether the defendant can be held liable for an act committed in another country.

Conditions of Liability for Foreign Tort
1. The Act Must Be Wrongful in the Foreign Country

• The act committed by the defendant must be considered wrongful under the law of the country where it was committed.

• The plaintiff must prove that the act gives rise to a legal claim in that country.

• If the act is not considered wrongful in that country, the claim may fail.

2. The Act Must Also Be Actionable in the Country Where the Case Is Filed

• The act must also be recognised as a legal wrong under the law of the country where the case is filed.

• This means that the act must be such that it could give rise to a legal action if committed in that country.

• This condition ensures that the court does not impose liability for an act that its own law does not recognise as a tort.

3. The Act Must Not Be Justified Under Foreign Law

• The defendant should not have a valid legal justification under the law of the country where the act was committed.

• If the act was legally authorised or justified in that country, the defendant may not be held liable under the traditional rule.

4. The Court Must Have Jurisdiction

• The court hearing the case must have legal authority to decide the dispute.

• The defendant must fall within the jurisdiction of that court according to the applicable legal rules.

5. The Plaintiff Must Prove Legal Injury

• The plaintiff must establish that they suffered a legally recognised injury because of the defendant's wrongful act.

• The plaintiff must also prove the connection between the wrongful act and the damage suffered.

6. No Valid Defence Should Be Available

• The defendant may use legally recognised defences, such as consent, necessity or other applicable defences.

• If the defendant successfully establishes a valid defence, liability may be avoided.

Important Case Law: Phillips v. Eyre (1870)

Facts: The defendant was the Governor of Jamaica. He was sued in England for certain acts committed in Jamaica during a rebellion. These acts had been authorised by a Jamaican law.

Judgment: The Court laid down the traditional rule for deciding whether an action could be brought in England for a tort committed in a foreign country.

Principle of Law: According to the traditional rule in Phillips v. Eyre, a foreign tort was actionable in England when:

1. The act was wrongful under the law of the country where it was committed.

2. The act would also have been wrongful if committed in England.

3. The act was not justified under the law of the foreign country.

Conclusion

Liability for foreign tort depends upon the legal rules of the country where the wrongful act was committed and the country where the case is filed. The traditional rule in Phillips v. Eyre (1870) provides two important requirements: the act must be wrongful in the foreign country and actionable in the country where the case is brought. These principles help courts decide disputes involving wrongful acts committed outside their own country.
