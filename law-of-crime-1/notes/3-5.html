Unsoundness of Mind

Law of Crimes – I · Topic 3.5

Unsoundness of mind is an important general exception in criminal law. The law recognises that a person may not be criminally responsible if, because of a serious mental condition, the person was unable to understand the nature of the act or that it was wrong or contrary to law.

Legal Provision – Section 22 BNS:

Section 22 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS), provides that an act is not an offence if, at the time of doing it, a person is, due to unsoundness of mind, incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that the act is either wrong or contrary to law. This provision corresponds to Section 84 of the former Indian Penal Code, 1860.

Difference between Medical and Legal Insanity:

Medical insanity is a broad clinical term used by psychiatrists and medical professionals to describe any form of mental disorder, disease, or psychological abnormality. It encompasses all types and degrees of mental instability diagnosed through medical science. However, the law does not excuse every form of medical insanity. A person may be medically insane, but unless their mental condition satisfies the specific legal test of affecting their cognitive faculties at the time of the crime, they remain criminally liable.

Legal insanity It refers to the mental condition of a person recognized by law as exempting them from criminal responsibility. Under criminal law (such as Section 22 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita), a person is legally insane only if, at the time of committing the act, they were suffering from a mental illness that rendered them incapable of understanding the nature of their act, or of knowing that what they were doing was wrong or contrary to law.

Important Tests of Unsoundness of Mind:
1. Wild Beast Test – R v. Arnold (1724)

Meaning: This historical test required an extreme degree of mental incapacity. The accused had to be so deprived of understanding and memory that the person did not know what they were doing, like an infant or a wild beast.

Principle: The test focused on the complete loss of reason and understanding. It was considered very strict and is not the test currently applied under Section 22 BNS.

2. M'Naghten Rules (1843)

Case: M'Naghten's Case (1843).

Meaning: Under these rules, a person may receive the defence of insanity if, due to a defect of reason caused by a disease of the mind, the person did not know the nature and quality of the act or did not know that the act was wrong.

Principle: The rules focus on the accused's ability to understand the act and its wrongfulness at the time of the offence. Section 22 BNS substantially follows this cognitive approach.

3. Irresistible Impulse Test

Meaning: This test examines whether a mental disorder made the accused unable to control or resist the impulse to commit the offence, even if the accused understood that the act was wrong.

Principle: It focuses on the ability to control conduct rather than only the ability to understand right and wrong. This is a comparative legal test used in certain jurisdictions; it is not a separate defence expressly provided under Section 22 BNS.

4. Durham or Product Test (1954)

Case: Durham v. United States (1954).

Meaning: Under this test, a person could be found legally insane if the unlawful act was the product of a mental disease or defect.

Principle: The test examines the connection between mental illness and criminal conduct. It was developed in the United States and is not the governing statutory test under Section 22 BNS.

5. Substantial Capacity Test

Meaning: This approach asks whether, because of a mental disease or defect, the accused lacked substantial capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of the conduct or to control the conduct according to the requirements of law.

Principle: It considers both the ability to understand and the ability to control behaviour. It is a comparative approach and does not replace the specific requirements of Section 22 BNS in India.

Important Indian Case Laws:
1. Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat (1964)

Facts: Dahyabhai was prosecuted for murder and raised the defence of insanity.

Judgment: The Supreme Court explained the burden of proof in cases involving a plea of insanity.

Principle: The accused must establish the circumstances supporting the general exception on the balance of probabilities. However, the prosecution must always prove the offence beyond reasonable doubt. Evidence of insanity may also create reasonable doubt about the required criminal intention.

2. Bapu alias Gajraj Singh v. State of Rajasthan (2007)

Facts: Bapu was convicted of a serious offence and claimed protection on the ground of unsoundness of mind.

Judgment: The Supreme Court rejected the defence because the evidence did not establish legal insanity at the time of the offence.

Principle: The Court distinguished medical insanity from legal insanity. The decisive question is whether, at the time of committing the act, the accused lacked the capacity specified by the law.

Conclusion:

Thus, unsoundness of mind is an important general exception under Section 22 BNS. The Wild Beast Test, M'Naghten Rules, Irresistible Impulse Test, Durham Test, and Substantial Capacity Test illustrate the historical development of legal approaches to insanity. However, in India, the applicable test is the statutory test under Section 22, which substantially follows the M'Naghten approach. The court must examine the accused's mental condition at the time of the offence and determine whether the legal requirements are satisfied.
