Module 03 · General Exceptions

Drunkenness

Law of Crimes – I · Topic 3.6

Introduction:

Intoxication means a condition in which a person's mental or physical abilities are affected by alcohol or another intoxicating substance. Under criminal law, intoxication may affect a person's ability to understand an act or form a particular intention. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) deals with intoxication under Sections 23 and 24.

1. Section 23 – Involuntary Intoxication:

Meaning: Section 23 provides a defence where, due to intoxication, a person is incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that the act is wrong or contrary to law, provided the intoxicating substance was administered without the person's knowledge or against their will.

Essential Conditions:

1. Involuntary Intoxication: The substance must have been administered without the person's knowledge or against their will.

2. Incapacity to Understand: The intoxication must make the person incapable of understanding the nature or wrongfulness of the act.

3. Condition at the Time of the Act: The required incapacity must exist when the act is committed.

Example: A secretly mixes an intoxicating substance into B's drink. B becomes incapable of understanding the nature of an act and commits it in that condition. If the statutory requirements are proved, B may receive protection under Section 23.

2. Section 24 – Voluntary Intoxication:

Meaning: Section 24 deals with offences requiring particular intention or knowledge. Generally, a person who voluntarily becomes intoxicated is treated as having the same knowledge as if they were sober, unless the intoxicating substance was administered without their knowledge or against their will.

Important Points:

1. Voluntary Intoxication: A person ordinarily cannot escape criminal liability merely by claiming that they were drunk.

2. Knowledge: The law generally attributes to an intoxicated person the knowledge they would have possessed while sober.

3. Intention: The court may examine the degree of intoxication and all surrounding circumstances when determining whether the required intention existed.

Example: A voluntarily drinks alcohol and then attacks B with a knife. A cannot automatically escape criminal liability by claiming that intoxication prevented them from thinking clearly.

Important Case Law: Basdev v. State of Pepsu (1956)

Facts: Basdev, a retired military officer, attended a wedding where alcohol was consumed. He asked a young boy, Maghar Singh, to move from his seat. When the boy did not move, Basdev took out his pistol and shot the boy in the abdomen. The boy died. Basdev argued that his intoxication should reduce his criminal responsibility.

Judgment: The Supreme Court of India held that Basdev's intoxication did not establish that he was incapable of forming the necessary intention. His conviction for murder was upheld, and his appeal was dismissed.

Principle of Judgment: The Court held that knowledge is generally attributed to an intoxicated person as if they were sober. Intention must be assessed from the circumstances, taking account of the degree of intoxication. Mere drunkenness or increased anger does not automatically remove criminal responsibility.

Conclusion:

Thus, intoxication is a limited defence under criminal law. Section 23 BNS protects a person in qualifying cases of involuntary intoxication, while Section 24 BNS generally prevents voluntary intoxication from being used to avoid the knowledge required for criminal liability. The court examines the circumstances and the person's actual mental condition when deciding whether the relevant legal requirements are fulfilled.
