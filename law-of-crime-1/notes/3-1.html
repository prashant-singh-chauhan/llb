Module 03 · General Exceptions

Mistake

Law of Crimes – I · Topic 3.1

Introduction:

The principle “Ignorance of law is no excuse, but a mistake of fact may be a good defence” is an important principle of criminal law. Generally, a person cannot escape criminal liability by claiming that they did not know the law. However, a person may receive protection if they act under a genuine mistake of fact in good faith and fulfil the legal requirements. This principle is recognised under Sections 14 and 17 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS).

1. Mistake of Law:

A mistake of law occurs when a person does not know the law or misunderstands its meaning. As a general rule, this is not a defence against criminal liability. A person cannot ordinarily avoid punishment merely by saying that they were unaware that their act was illegal.

Example: A person sells a prohibited item and claims that they did not know the law prohibited its sale. Mere ignorance of the law does not ordinarily excuse the offence.

2. Section 14 BNS – Act Done by a Person Bound by Law:

Meaning: Section 14 provides that an act is not an offence when a person is legally bound to perform it or, because of a mistake of fact and not a mistake of law, honestly and in good faith believes that the law requires the act.

Illustration: A court officer receives an order to arrest Y. After making proper enquiries, the officer mistakenly believes that Z is Y and arrests Z. The officer may receive protection under Section 14 because the mistake concerns the person's identity, which is a matter of fact.

Similarly, a soldier who fires on a mob in accordance with a lawful superior order, as required by law, commits no offence under the statutory illustration.

3. Section 17 BNS – Act Done by a Person Justified by Law:

Meaning: Section 17 provides protection when a person is justified by law in performing an act or, because of a mistake of fact and not a mistake of law, believes in good faith that the act is legally justified.

Illustration: A sees Z committing what appears to be murder. Believing in good faith that Z has committed murder, A apprehends Z and takes steps to bring Z before the proper authorities. If Z was actually acting in self-defence, A may still be protected if the legal conditions are satisfied.

4. Requirement of Good Faith:

The defence of mistake of fact requires good faith. A person must act honestly and with due care and attention appropriate to the circumstances. A careless or dishonest claim of factual mistake may not receive legal protection.

Important Case Law: State of Orissa v. Ram Bahadur Thapa (1959)

Facts: Ram Bahadur Thapa attacked certain people because he mistakenly believed that they were ghosts. One person died and others suffered injuries.

Judgment: The Orissa High Court upheld his acquittal under Section 79 of the former Indian Penal Code, 1860.

Principle: A genuine mistake of fact may provide a defence when the person acts in good faith. The court considered the accused's belief and the circumstances of the incident. Section 79 IPC corresponds to Section 17 BNS.

Conclusion:

Thus, ignorance of law is generally no excuse, whereas a genuine mistake of fact may provide a defence under Sections 14 and 17 BNS. Section 14 deals with acts believed to be required by law, while Section 17 deals with acts believed to be justified by law. In both cases, the mistake must concern facts, not the law, and the statutory requirement of good faith must be fulfilled.
