Module 01 · General Principles of Crime

Concept of Strict Liability

Law of Crimes – I · Topic 1.4

Introduction:

Strict liability is a legal principle under which a person may be held legally responsible for an act or offence even without proof of a guilty mind (mens rea). It is an exception to the general rule of criminal law that both a wrongful act and the required guilty mind must be proved. Strict liability applies only where the law provides for it.

Meaning of Strict Liability:

Strict liability means liability without the need to prove a particular criminal intention or guilty mind. The prosecution must still establish the prohibited act and the other elements required by the relevant law. It does not mean that a person is automatically guilty whenever harm occurs.

Essential Features of Strict Liability:

1. Absence of Mens Rea: The prosecution need not prove a guilty mind for the element of the offence to which strict liability applies. The physical act or prohibited conduct must still be established.

2. Presence of Actus Reus: The prohibited act or omission (actus reus) must be proved. A person cannot be convicted merely because an undesirable result has occurred.

3. Application of Law: Strict liability applies when the law expressly excludes mens rea or does so by necessary implication. Courts examine the wording, purpose, and nature of the relevant law.

4. Public Welfare: Strict liability is often used for certain regulatory offences concerning public safety, health, trade, and similar matters. It encourages people to follow legal duties carefully.

5. Limited Defences: The availability of a defence depends on the relevant law. A person cannot always escape liability merely by showing that the act was unintentional or that reasonable care was taken.

Major Judicial Cases:
1. Rylands v. Fletcher (1868)

Facts: The defendant, Rylands, constructed a reservoir on his land to supply water to his mill. Water escaped through old mine shafts and flooded the plaintiff Fletcher's working mine. The defendant had not personally been negligent.

Judgment: The House of Lords held the defendant liable for the damage caused by the escape of water, even without proof of negligence.

Principle of Judgment: This case established the rule of strict liability. A person who brings and collects a dangerous thing on their land for a non-natural use may be liable if it escapes and causes damage. However, certain exceptions, such as an act of God or the plaintiff's own fault, may apply.

2. State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965)

Facts: Mayer Hans George, a German national, travelled by air carrying approximately 34 kilograms of gold concealed on his person. Indian regulations required the necessary permission for bringing gold into India. He claimed that he did not know about the relevant notification.

Judgment: The Supreme Court of India, by majority, restored his conviction and held that knowledge of the notification was not necessary for the offence in the circumstances of the case.

Principle of Judgment: This case established that mens rea is not essential in every statutory offence. A law may exclude the requirement of a guilty mind expressly or by necessary implication.

3. M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987)

Facts: In December 1985, oleum gas leaked from the Shriram industrial complex in Delhi, causing death and injuries. The incident raised serious concerns about the safety of hazardous industries.

Judgment: The Supreme Court of India held that enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities owe an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community. Such enterprises must compensate victims for harm caused by their activities.

Principle of Judgment: This case established the principle of absolute liability in India. Unlike ordinary strict liability under Rylands v. Fletcher, absolute liability does not permit the traditional exceptions available under that rule.

Strict Liability in Tort Law:

Strict liability also applies in law of torts. In Rylands v. Fletcher (1868), a person was held liable for damage caused by the escape of a dangerous thing brought onto land for a non-natural use, subject to recognised exceptions. In India, the Supreme Court developed the stronger principle of absolute liability for hazardous industries in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987).

Conclusion:

Thus, strict liability is an important exception to the general requirement of mens rea. It helps enforce certain legal duties effectively while ensuring that liability is imposed according to the applicable law. Its scope depends on the nature of the offence and the relevant statutory provisions.
