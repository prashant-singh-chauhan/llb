1. Solitary Confinement under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
1. Introduction

Solitary confinement means keeping a prisoner separately from other prisoners in a place of isolation. It is a severe form of punishment because it restricts the prisoner's social contact. Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, Sections 11 and 12 deal with solitary confinement and prescribe limits on its duration and manner of execution. These provisions aim to prevent the excessive use of solitary confinement.

2. Section 11: Solitary Confinement

Section 11 empowers a court to order solitary confinement when a person is convicted of an offence punishable with rigorous imprisonment. Rigorous imprisonment means imprisonment involving hard labour. Solitary confinement cannot be ordered as part of a sentence of simple imprisonment.

The maximum period of solitary confinement depends upon the total term of imprisonment:

Imprisonment up to six months: Solitary confinement may not exceed one month.

Imprisonment exceeding six months but not exceeding one year: It may not exceed two months.

Imprisonment exceeding one year: It may not exceed three months in the whole.

Thus, solitary confinement is not a separate punishment imposed independently. It is an additional measure that a court may order for a limited part of a sentence of rigorous imprisonment.

3. Section 12: Limits on Solitary Confinement

Section 12 lays down rules regarding the execution of solitary confinement. These rules protect prisoners from being kept in isolation for long and continuous periods.

Maximum period at a time: Solitary confinement cannot exceed fourteen days at a time.

Mandatory intervals: After a period of solitary confinement, the prisoner must be allowed an interval of at least equal duration before another period of solitary confinement begins.

Longer imprisonment: Where the total sentence of imprisonment exceeds three months, solitary confinement cannot exceed seven days in any one month of the entire imprisonment. The prescribed intervals must also be observed.

These restrictions ensure that solitary confinement is imposed only within the limits established by law.

4. Illustrations

Illustration 1: A person is sentenced to one year of rigorous imprisonment. Under Section 11, solitary confinement may be ordered for a maximum of two months. However, it must be carried out according to the restrictions prescribed under Section 12.

Illustration 2: A person is sentenced to ten years of rigorous imprisonment. The total solitary confinement cannot exceed three months. Further, the prisoner cannot be kept in solitary confinement for more than seven days in any one month of the entire imprisonment.

5. Importance of Legal Restrictions

Solitary confinement can seriously affect a prisoner's physical and mental well-being. Continuous isolation may cause emotional distress and psychological difficulties. Therefore, the law places strict limits on its duration and execution.

Sections 11 and 12 maintain a balance between the power of the court to punish offenders and the need to protect prisoners from excessive isolation. They ensure that solitary confinement is used only in accordance with legal requirements.

6. Conclusion

Solitary confinement is an exceptional measure under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Section 11 specifies when and for how long it may be imposed, while Section 12 regulates its execution through maximum periods and mandatory intervals. Together, these provisions prevent excessive solitary confinement and promote the lawful and controlled treatment of prisoners.

2. What Do You Mean by ‘Dowry Death’?
1. Introduction

Dowry death is a serious social and legal problem in India. It refers to the death of a woman caused by burns, bodily injury, or other circumstances that are not normal, where the death occurs within seven years of her marriage and it is shown that she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or his relatives in connection with a demand for dowry shortly before her death.

The law relating to dowry death aims to protect married women from harassment and violence arising from unlawful demands for dowry.

2. Legal Provision

Under Section 80 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, dowry death is a punishable offence. This provision replaces the corresponding provision under Section 304B of the Indian Penal Code, 1860.

According to Section 80, where the death of a woman is caused by burns or bodily injury, or occurs otherwise than under normal circumstances, within seven years of her marriage, and it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or his relative for, or in connection with, a demand for dowry, such death is called a dowry death.

3. Essential Elements of Dowry Death

The following conditions must be established to constitute dowry death:

1. Death of a Woman: The victim must be a married woman who has died.

2. Unnatural Death: Her death must be caused by burns, bodily injury, or circumstances other than normal death.

3. Within Seven Years of Marriage: The death must occur within seven years of her marriage.

4. Cruelty or Harassment: The woman must have been subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or his relatives.

5. Connection with Dowry Demand: The cruelty or harassment must be related to a demand for dowry.

6. Soon Before Death: The cruelty or harassment connected with the dowry demand must have occurred soon before her death. This requirement establishes a link between the harassment and the death.

4. Punishment for Dowry Death

Section 80(2) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, provides that a person who commits dowry death shall be punished with imprisonment for a term of not less than seven years, which may extend to imprisonment for life.

The punishment is severe because dowry-related cruelty and deaths are serious offences that threaten the safety and dignity of married women.

5. Presumption Regarding Dowry Death

Section 118 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, deals with the presumption as to dowry death. When it is shown that soon before her death, a woman was subjected to cruelty or harassment in connection with a dowry demand by her husband or his relative, the Court shall presume that such person caused the dowry death, subject to the legal requirements of the provision.

This rule helps protect women by addressing the difficulty of proving what happens inside the matrimonial home.

6. Example

Suppose a woman dies of burns within five years of her marriage. Evidence shows that her husband and in-laws repeatedly harassed her for bringing additional money as dowry shortly before her death. If the legal requirements are established, the death may be treated as dowry death under Section 80 of the BNS, 2023.

7. Conclusion

Dowry death is a grave offence that reflects the harmful effects of dowry-related cruelty and harassment. Section 80 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, provides strict punishment for this offence, while Section 118 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, provides an important evidentiary presumption. These provisions aim to protect married women, discourage dowry-related violence, and promote justice and equality in society.

3. What Do You Mean by ‘Mischief’?
1. Introduction

Mischief is an offence against property. It refers to intentionally or knowingly causing wrongful loss or damage to another person or to the public by destroying property or changing its condition in a way that reduces its value or usefulness. The law punishes such acts to protect property and prevent people from causing unlawful damage to others.

Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, the offence of mischief is defined under Section 324.

2. Definition of Mischief

According to Section 324 of the BNS, a person commits mischief if, with the intention of causing, or knowing that they are likely to cause, wrongful loss or damage to the public or to any person, they cause the destruction of property or any change in property or its situation that destroys or diminishes its value or utility, or affects it injuriously.

In simple words, mischief means deliberately damaging or changing property in a manner that causes wrongful loss or reduces its value or usefulness.

3. Essential Elements of Mischief

The following are the essential elements of the offence of mischief:

1. Intention or Knowledge: The accused must intend to cause wrongful loss or damage, or know that such loss or damage is likely to occur.

2. Destruction or Change in Property: The accused must destroy property or cause a change in the property or its situation.

3. Reduction in Value or Utility: The destruction or change must destroy or reduce the value or usefulness of the property, or affect it injuriously.

4. Wrongful Loss or Damage: The act must be intended or known to be likely to cause wrongful loss or damage to the public or another person.

5. Property May Belong to Another Person or to the Accused: Mischief may be committed even where the property belongs to the accused, if the act causes or is likely to cause wrongful loss or damage to another person or the public. Ownership alone does not excuse the act.

4. Illustrations of Mischief

Illustration 1: A person intentionally breaks the windows of another person's house. The act reduces the value and usefulness of the property and causes wrongful loss. Therefore, it may amount to mischief.

Illustration 2: A person deliberately damages a public water pipeline, preventing people from receiving water. Such an act may amount to mischief because it damages property and causes loss to the public.

Illustration 3: A person intentionally kills another person's animal without lawful justification. Depending on the facts and applicable provisions, the act may amount to mischief.

5. Punishment for Mischief

Section 324 of the BNS provides punishment for mischief. Under Section 324(2), a person who commits mischief may be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term extending to six months, or with a fine, or with both. Where the mischief causes greater loss or damage, the punishment may be more severe under the relevant subsections of Section 324.

6. Conclusion

Mischief is an important offence under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, because it protects private and public property from intentional or knowing damage. Section 324 defines the offence and provides punishment according to the nature and seriousness of the act. Its main purpose is to prevent wrongful loss, protect property rights, and maintain public order.

4. What is Cheating by Personation
1. Introduction

Cheating by personation is a form of cheating in which a person deceives another by pretending to be someone else, knowingly substituting one person for another, or representing that they or another person is someone different from who they actually are. It is a criminal offence because it involves deception and may cause wrongful loss to another person.

Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, cheating by personation is dealt with under Section 319.

2. Definition of Cheating by Personation

According to Section 319(1) of the BNS, a person commits cheating by personation if they cheat by pretending to be some other person, knowingly substituting one person for another, or representing that they or another person is someone other than who they really are.

In simple words, cheating by personation means deceiving someone by falsely claiming to be another person or by using another person's identity to obtain an unfair advantage.

3. Essential Elements of Cheating by Personation

The following are the essential elements of this offence:

1. Deception: The accused must deceive another person through a false representation or identity.

2. False Identity: The accused must pretend to be another person, knowingly substitute one person for another, or falsely represent the identity of themselves or another person.

3. Dishonest or Fraudulent Conduct: The act must amount to cheating under the law. Mere use of a false name, without the necessary elements of cheating, does not automatically establish the offence.

4. Intention to Deceive: The accused must act with the intention required to constitute cheating. The circumstances and purpose of the false identity are important in determining criminal liability.

4. Illustrations of Cheating by Personation

Illustration 1: A person pretends to be a bank officer and asks another person to provide confidential banking information to obtain money dishonestly. This conduct may amount to cheating by personation.

Illustration 2: A person appears for an examination by falsely claiming to be a registered candidate and using that candidate's identity. This may amount to cheating by personation, depending on the facts and applicable law.

Illustration 3: A person falsely claims to be a government officer to induce another person to pay money. If the required elements of cheating are established, the conduct may constitute cheating by personation.

5. Punishment for Cheating by Personation

Under Section 319(2) of the BNS, 2023, a person who commits cheating by personation is punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term that may extend to five years, or with a fine, or with both.

The punishment aims to discourage identity-based deception and protect individuals from fraud.

6. Difference Between Cheating and Cheating by Personation

Cheating: It involves deception that induces a person to deliver property, consent to its retention, or act or omit to act in a way that causes or is likely to cause harm, as defined by law.

Cheating by Personation: It is a specific form of cheating in which deception is carried out by falsely assuming or representing an identity.

7. Conclusion

Cheating by personation is a serious offence under Section 319 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. It protects people against deception through false identities and dishonest representations. The law provides punishment to discourage such conduct and promote trust and security in personal, commercial, and public dealings.

5. Differentiate Between Theft and Misappropriation
1. Introduction

Theft and dishonest misappropriation of property are offences against property under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023. Both offences involve dishonest conduct and wrongful gain or loss. However, they differ in the manner in which property is obtained and dishonestly dealt with.

Theft is defined under Section 303 of the BNS, whereas dishonest misappropriation of property is defined under Section 314 of the BNS.

2. Meaning of Theft

Under Section 303 of the BNS, a person commits theft if they, intending to take dishonestly any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person's consent, move that property in order to such taking.

In simple words, theft means dishonestly taking movable property out of another person's possession without consent.

Example: A person secretly takes another person's mobile phone from a table without permission and carries it away. This act may amount to theft.

3. Meaning of Dishonest Misappropriation

Under Section 314 of the BNS, a person commits dishonest misappropriation of property if they dishonestly misappropriate or convert movable property to their own use.

In simple words, misappropriation occurs when a person dishonestly uses or treats property as their own, even though they have no lawful right to do so.

Example: A person finds a lost wallet and, knowing or having the means to discover the owner, dishonestly keeps the money for personal use. Depending on the circumstances and the statutory requirements, this may amount to dishonest misappropriation.

6. Legal Insanity and Medical Insanity
1. Introduction

Insanity is a condition that may affect a person's mental ability to understand their actions. In criminal law, the distinction between legal insanity and medical insanity is important because not every person suffering from a mental illness is exempt from criminal responsibility.

Under Section 22 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, an act is not an offence if, at the time of doing it, the person, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that what they are doing is either wrong or contrary to law.

2. Meaning of Medical Insanity

Medical insanity refers to a mental illness or psychological disorder identified from a medical point of view. It is diagnosed by qualified medical professionals through examination of the person's mental condition, symptoms, behaviour, and medical history.

A person may suffer from a mental disorder but still understand the nature and consequences of their actions. Therefore, medical insanity alone does not automatically provide a defence against criminal liability.

Example: A person suffers from a mental disorder but understands that stealing another person's property is wrong and knowingly commits theft. The person cannot automatically claim exemption from criminal responsibility merely because of the medical condition.

3. Meaning of Legal Insanity

Legal insanity is a legal concept used to determine whether a person can be held criminally responsible for an act. Under Section 22 of the BNS, the defence applies when, at the time of committing the act, unsoundness of mind makes the person incapable of understanding the nature of the act or that the act is wrong or contrary to law.

The focus is not simply on whether the person has a mental illness. The main question is whether the person's mental condition affected their understanding in the specific manner required by law at the time of the act.

Example: If, because of unsoundness of mind, a person is incapable of understanding the nature of an act at the time of committing it, the legal defence under Section 22 may apply if the required conditions are established.

6. Conclusion

Medical insanity concerns the diagnosis of mental illness, whereas legal insanity concerns criminal responsibility. A person may be medically ill but legally responsible for their actions. Under Section 22 of the BNS, 2023, exemption is available only when the statutory requirements relating to unsoundness of mind are satisfied at the time of the act. Thus, the distinction is essential for ensuring fairness in criminal justice.

7. Actus Non Facit Reum Nisi Mens Sit Rea
1. Introduction

Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea is a Latin legal maxim which means that an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty. In simple words, a person generally cannot be held criminally responsible merely for doing a prohibited act; the required guilty intention or mental element must also be present.

This maxim expresses an important principle of criminal law. It distinguishes an innocent mistake or accidental act from an act committed with a blameworthy state of mind. However, the requirement of a guilty mind depends on the definition of the particular offence and is not necessary in every criminal offence.

2. Meaning of Actus Reus and Mens Rea

Actus Reus: It means the physical element of a crime. It may include an act, an unlawful omission, or a prohibited consequence, depending on the offence.

Mens Rea: It means the guilty mind or the mental element required by law. It may involve intention, knowledge, dishonesty, recklessness, or another specified mental state.

For most traditional criminal offences, the prosecution must establish both the prohibited conduct and the required mental element. The two elements must be connected in the manner required by law.

3. Importance of the Maxim

The maxim is important for the following reasons:

1. Protection of Innocent Persons: It helps prevent the punishment of people who perform an act without the guilty mental state required for the offence.

2. Fairness in Criminal Justice: It recognises that a person's intention and knowledge may be relevant when deciding criminal responsibility.

3. Difference Between Accident and Crime: An accidental act may not constitute an offence requiring intention or knowledge, although other legal rules may apply.

4. Proper Assessment of Criminal Liability: Courts examine the facts, circumstances, conduct, and available evidence to determine whether the required mental element existed.

4. Illustrations

Illustration 1: Accidental Damage

A person accidentally breaks another person's window while playing a game, without intending or knowing that such damage is likely. If the required dishonest or other guilty mental element is absent, the act may not amount to the offence of mischief.

Illustration 2: Intentional Theft

A person knowingly takes another person's mobile phone without consent, intending to keep it dishonestly. Here, the physical act of taking the phone and the required dishonest intention may together establish theft.

Illustration 3: Mistaken Identity

A person takes an umbrella believing it to be their own. If the person honestly makes a mistake and lacks the dishonest intention required for theft, criminal liability for theft may not arise.

5. Exceptions to the Maxim

The maxim is a general principle and does not apply in the same way to every offence. In certain offences, known as strict-liability offences, the prosecution may not need to prove a guilty mental state for a particular element of the offence. Such liability depends on the wording of the relevant law.

Therefore, the absence of intention does not automatically provide a defence in every criminal case. The court must examine the legal requirements of the specific offence.

6. Conclusion

The maxim Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea means that an act alone does not ordinarily make a person criminally guilty unless the required guilty mind is also present. It is a fundamental principle of criminal law that promotes fairness and protects innocent persons from unjust punishment. However, its application depends on the nature and statutory requirements of the offence.

8. Cheating under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
1. Introduction

Cheating is an offence against property that involves deceiving a person dishonestly or fraudulently. It occurs when a person uses deception to induce another person to deliver property, allow the retention of property, or do or omit something that causes or is likely to cause harm. Cheating is punishable under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023.

2. Definition of Cheating

Section 318(1) of the BNS, 2023 defines cheating. A person cheats when, by deceiving another person, they fraudulently or dishonestly induce that person to deliver property to someone, consent to the retention of property, or intentionally induce that person to do or omit something that they would not otherwise do or omit, where such act or omission causes or is likely to cause damage or harm to that person in body, mind, reputation, or property.

In simple words, cheating means deceiving someone to obtain an unfair benefit or to cause harm to another person.

3. Essential Elements of Cheating

The following are the essential elements of cheating:

1. Deception: The accused must deceive another person through a false representation or concealment of facts where the law recognises it as deception.

2. Fraudulent or Dishonest Intention: The accused must have the required intention to deceive dishonestly or fraudulently, as applicable to the act.

3. Inducement: The deception must induce the victim to deliver property, consent to its retention, or do or omit something they would not otherwise do or omit.

4. Damage or Harm: The induced act or omission must cause, or be likely to cause, harm to the victim in body, mind, reputation, or property.

5. Intention at the Relevant Time: In cases involving dishonest inducement to deliver property, the dishonest intention must exist when the inducement is made. A mere failure to fulfil a promise does not automatically amount to cheating.

4. Illustrations of Cheating

Illustration 1: A person falsely claims to own a valuable vehicle and dishonestly induces another person to pay money for it. This may amount to cheating if the required elements are established.

Illustration 2: A person knowingly sells imitation jewellery as genuine gold and receives money from the buyer. The false representation may constitute cheating.

Illustration 3: A person borrows money with a genuine intention to repay it but later faces financial difficulties. The failure to repay, by itself, does not establish cheating. The original intention and surrounding circumstances must be examined.

5. Punishment for Cheating

Section 318(2): A person who cheats is punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term that may extend to three years, or with a fine, or with both.

Section 318(3): Where a person cheats while knowing that they are likely to cause wrongful loss to a person whose interest they are legally or contractually bound to protect, the punishment may extend to five years of imprisonment, or a fine, or both.

Section 318(4): Where cheating dishonestly induces a person to deliver property or to make, alter, or destroy a valuable security, the punishment may extend to seven years of imprisonment and a fine.

6. Conclusion

Cheating is an important offence under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Section 318 protects individuals against dishonest deception and fraud. To establish cheating, the required deception, intention, and inducement must be proved according to the nature of the case. The law aims to protect property, reputation, and personal interests and to promote honesty in social and commercial dealings.

9. Abettor under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
1. Introduction

An abettor is a person who encourages, helps, or intentionally assists another person in committing an offence. The law punishes abetment because a person who intentionally supports a crime may also be held criminally responsible.

2. Definition of Abettor

Under Section 45 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, a person abets the doing of a thing by instigating another person to do it, engaging in a conspiracy to do it where an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy, or intentionally aiding its commission by an act or illegal omission.

A person who abets an offence is known as an abettor.

3. Essential Elements of Abetment

1. Instigation: Encouraging, provoking, or urging another person to commit an offence.

2. Conspiracy: Participating in a plan to commit an offence, along with the required act or illegal omission in pursuance of the conspiracy.

3. Intentional Aid: Intentionally helping or facilitating the commission of an offence.

4. Example

A encourages B to commit theft and intentionally provides tools to help B carry it out. Depending on the facts, A may be held liable as an abettor.

5. Conclusion

An abettor plays a supporting role in the commission of an offence. Sections 45 onwards of the BNS deal with abetment and its consequences. The law aims to hold persons accountable when they intentionally encourage, plan, or assist criminal acts.

10. Stalking
1. Introduction

Stalking is a form of harassment in which a person repeatedly follows, contacts, or monitors another person against their wishes. It may cause fear, mental stress, and insecurity. Stalking can take place in person or through electronic communication, such as the internet and social media. To protect women from such harassment, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, provides punishment for stalking.

2. Definition of Stalking

Section 78 of the BNS, 2023 deals with the offence of stalking. A man commits stalking if he follows a woman and contacts, or attempts to contact, her repeatedly to foster personal interaction despite a clear indication of disinterest by the woman, or monitors the use by a woman of the internet, email, or any other form of electronic communication.

However, the law recognises certain exceptions, including conduct that is undertaken to prevent or detect crime under the responsibility of the State, conduct required by law, and conduct that is reasonable and justified in the particular circumstances.

3. Essential Elements of Stalking

1. Following a Woman: The accused follows a woman repeatedly with the intention of establishing personal interaction despite her clear disinterest.

2. Repeated Contact: The accused repeatedly contacts or attempts to contact the woman despite her clear indication that she does not wish to communicate.

3. Monitoring Electronic Communication: Stalking may also involve monitoring a woman's use of the internet, email, or other forms of electronic communication.

4. Absence of a Recognised Exception: The conduct must not fall within the exceptions provided under Section 78.

4. Punishment for Stalking

Under Section 78(2) of the BNS, 2023, the punishment for stalking is as follows:

First conviction: Imprisonment for a term that may extend to three years, along with a fine.

Subsequent conviction: Imprisonment for a term that may extend to five years, along with a fine.

The law provides a stricter punishment for a person who commits the offence again after a previous conviction.

5. Illustrations

Illustration 1: A man repeatedly follows a woman to her college and continuously tries to speak to her despite her clearly refusing contact. Depending on the facts, this conduct may amount to stalking.

Illustration 2: A person repeatedly monitors a woman's online activities and electronic communications in circumstances covered by Section 78. Such conduct may constitute stalking.

6. Importance of the Law

The law against stalking protects women's privacy, personal freedom, dignity, and safety. It discourages unwanted repeated contact and the misuse of electronic communication. It also provides a legal remedy against behaviour that may cause fear or mental distress.

7. Conclusion

Stalking is a punishable offence under Section 78 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. It includes certain forms of repeated unwanted contact and monitoring of electronic communication. By prescribing punishment and recognising limited exceptions, the law seeks to protect women from harassment while ensuring that legitimate and legally justified conduct is not treated as an offence.

11. Movable Property
1. Introduction

Movable property is an important concept in criminal law, particularly in offences such as theft, misappropriation, and mischief. It refers to property that can generally be moved from one place to another.

2. Definition of Movable Property

According to Section 2(21) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, the term “movable property” is intended to include corporeal property of every description, except land and things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth.

In simple words, movable property includes physical objects that can be moved, such as money, jewellery, books, vehicles, and furniture.

3. Examples of Movable Property

1. Money: Cash and coins are movable property.

2. Jewellery: Gold, silver, and ornaments are movable property.

3. Vehicles: Cars, motorcycles, and bicycles are movable property.

4. Electronic Items: Mobile phones, laptops, and televisions are movable property.

4. Importance in Criminal Law

The concept of movable property is important because theft under Section 303 of the BNS involves dishonestly moving movable property out of another person's possession without consent. It is also relevant to dishonest misappropriation under Section 314.

5. Conclusion

Movable property means physical property that is not land or something attached to the earth in the manner excluded by law. Its definition helps determine whether certain property-related offences can be established under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

12. Injury
1. Introduction

Injury is an important concept in criminal law. It refers to harm caused to a person's body, mind, reputation, or property. The law punishes a person who unlawfully causes injury to another person.

2. Definition of Injury

According to Section 2(14) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, the term “injury” denotes any harm whatever illegally caused to any person, in body, mind, reputation, or property.

In simple words, injury means any unlawful harm caused to a person physically, mentally, to their reputation, or to their property.

3. Types of Injury

1. Bodily Injury: Harm caused to the body, such as cuts, bruises, or fractures.

2. Mental Injury: Harm caused to a person's mental well-being through unlawful acts.

3. Injury to Reputation: Harm caused to a person's good name or social standing.

4. Injury to Property: Unlawful damage or loss caused to another person's property.

4. Importance of Injury

The concept of injury helps determine whether an unlawful act has caused legally recognised harm. It is relevant to several offences under the BNS, depending on the facts and the applicable legal provision.

5. Conclusion

Thus, injury under Section 2(14) of the BNS includes unlawful harm to the body, mind, reputation, or property of a person. It is a broad concept that protects individuals against different forms of unlawful harm.

13. Strict Liability

Strict liability is a legal principle under which a person may be held legally responsible for an act or offence even without proof of a guilty mind (mens rea). It is an exception to the general rule of criminal law that both a wrongful act and the required guilty mind must be proved. Strict liability applies only where the law provides for it.

Meaning of Strict Liability:

Strict liability means liability without the need to prove a particular criminal intention or guilty mind. The prosecution must still establish the prohibited act and the other elements required by the relevant law. It does not mean that a person is automatically guilty whenever harm occurs.

Essential Features of Strict Liability:

1. Absence of Mens Rea: The prosecution need not prove a guilty mind for the element of the offence to which strict liability applies. The physical act or prohibited conduct must still be established.

2. Presence of Actus Reus: The prohibited act or omission (actus reus) must be proved. A person cannot be convicted merely because an undesirable result has occurred.

3. Application of Law: Strict liability applies when the law expressly excludes mens rea or does so by necessary implication. Courts examine the wording, purpose, and nature of the relevant law.

4. Public Welfare: Strict liability is often used for certain regulatory offences concerning public safety, health, trade, and similar matters. It encourages people to follow legal duties carefully.

5. Limited Defences: The availability of a defence depends on the relevant law. A person cannot always escape liability merely by showing that the act was unintentional or that reasonable care was taken.

Major Judicial Cases:
1. Rylands v. Fletcher (1868)

Facts: The defendant, Rylands, constructed a reservoir on his land to supply water to his mill. Water escaped through old mine shafts and flooded the plaintiff Fletcher's working mine. The defendant had not personally been negligent.

Judgment: The House of Lords held the defendant liable for the damage caused by the escape of water, even without proof of negligence.

Principle of Judgment: This case established the rule of strict liability. A person who brings and collects a dangerous thing on their land for a non-natural use may be liable if it escapes and causes damage. However, certain exceptions, such as an act of God or the plaintiff's own fault, may apply.

2. State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965)

Facts: Mayer Hans George, a German national, travelled by air carrying approximately 34 kilograms of gold concealed on his person. Indian regulations required the necessary permission for bringing gold into India. He claimed that he did not know about the relevant notification.

Judgment: The Supreme Court of India, by majority, restored his conviction and held that knowledge of the notification was not necessary for the offence in the circumstances of the case.

Principle of Judgment: This case established that mens rea is not essential in every statutory offence. A law may exclude the requirement of a guilty mind expressly or by necessary implication.

3. M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987)

Facts: In December 1985, oleum gas leaked from the Shriram industrial complex in Delhi, causing death and injuries. The incident raised serious concerns about the safety of hazardous industries.

Judgment: The Supreme Court of India held that enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities owe an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community. Such enterprises must compensate victims for harm caused by their activities.

Principle of Judgment: This case established the principle of absolute liability in India. Unlike ordinary strict liability under Rylands v. Fletcher, absolute liability does not permit the traditional exceptions available under that rule.

Strict Liability in Tort Law:

Strict liability also applies in law of torts. In Rylands v. Fletcher (1868), a person was held liable for damage caused by the escape of a dangerous thing brought onto land for a non-natural use, subject to recognised exceptions. In India, the Supreme Court developed the stronger principle of absolute liability for hazardous industries in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987).

Conclusion:

Thus, strict liability is an important exception to the general requirement of mens rea. It helps enforce certain legal duties effectively while ensuring that liability is imposed according to the applicable law. Its scope depends on the nature of the offence and the relevant statutory provisions.

17. Grievous Hurt
1. Introduction

Hurt is an important concept in criminal law. When an injury is serious in nature and falls within the categories specified by law, it is called grievous hurt. The law provides punishment for causing grievous hurt to protect the life and physical safety of individuals.

2. Meaning and Definition

Under Section 116 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, grievous hurt includes the following kinds of hurt:

1. Emasculation: Depriving a person of male sexual capacity.

2. Permanent loss of sight: Permanently destroying the sight of either eye.

3. Permanent loss of hearing: Permanently destroying the hearing of either ear.

4. Loss of a member or joint: Permanently causing the loss of a body part or joint.

5. Destruction or permanent impairment of a member or joint: Permanently damaging its function.

6. Permanent disfiguration of the head or face: Causing lasting damage to the appearance of the head or face.

7. Fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth: Breaking or dislocating a bone or tooth.

8. Any hurt endangering life: Causing an injury that puts life in danger, or causing severe bodily pain or inability to follow ordinary activities for fifteen days.

3. Essential Features

For an injury to amount to grievous hurt, it must fall within one of the categories mentioned in Section 116. An injury may be serious in ordinary language but will not legally be grievous hurt unless it meets the statutory requirements.

4. Punishment

Under Section 117 of the BNS, 2023, voluntarily causing grievous hurt is generally punishable with imprisonment up to seven years and a fine. Special provisions apply in certain circumstances, including grievous hurt causing permanent disability or a persistent vegetative state, and grievous hurt by dangerous weapons or means is separately covered by Section 118.

5. Example

If A deliberately strikes B and breaks B's arm, the injury may amount to grievous hurt because fracture of a bone is expressly included in Section 116.

6. Conclusion

Grievous hurt means serious injury falling within the categories specified by law. Sections 116 and 117 of the BNS help identify such injuries and provide punishment for causing them.

18. Criminal Force
1. Introduction

Criminal force is an offence against the body of a person. It involves the intentional use of force without consent for an unlawful purpose. The law protects individuals from the improper use of physical force against them.

2. Meaning and Definition

Under Section 129 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, force is used when a person causes motion, change of motion, or cessation of motion to another person, or causes a substance to come into contact with another person's body, clothing, or something they carry, in the circumstances specified by law.

Under Section 131, criminal force is committed when a person intentionally uses force against another without that person's consent, in order to commit an offence or with the intention, or knowledge of likely consequences, of causing injury, fear, or annoyance to that person.

3. Essential Elements

1. Use of force: There must be the use of force as understood under Section 129.

2. Intention: The force must be used intentionally.

3. Absence of consent: The force must be used without the person's consent.

4. Unlawful purpose or consequence: The force must be used to commit an offence or with the required intention or knowledge of causing injury, fear, or annoyance.

4. Examples

If A deliberately pushes B without consent to frighten or annoy B, A may commit criminal force, depending on the circumstances.

If A accidentally bumps into B in a crowded place without the required intention or knowledge, it would not ordinarily amount to criminal force.

5. Punishment

Under Section 131 of the BNS, 2023, a person who commits assault or uses criminal force otherwise than on grave and sudden provocation may be punished with imprisonment up to three months, a fine up to ₹1,000, or both. Specific circumstances may attract other provisions and punishments.

6. Conclusion

Criminal force means intentionally using force without consent in circumstances covered by law. Sections 129 and 131 of the BNS explain the concept and provide punishment for its unlawful use.

19. Mistake of Fact
1. Introduction

Mistake of fact is an important general exception in criminal law. Sometimes, a person performs an act because they honestly believe certain facts to be true. If the law's conditions are satisfied, the person may be protected from criminal liability.

2. Legal Provision

Section 14 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023 protects an act done by a person who is bound by law to do it, or who, because of a mistake of fact and not a mistake of law, in good faith believes themselves to be bound by law to do it.

Section 17 also protects an act done by a person who, because of a mistake of fact and not a mistake of law, in good faith believes themselves to be justified by law in doing it.

3. Meaning of Mistake of Fact

A mistake of fact occurs when a person has an incorrect understanding of a factual situation. A mistake of law occurs when a person misunderstands the law. Generally, a mistake of law is not a defence under these provisions.

4. Essential Conditions

1. Mistake of fact: The person's belief must arise from an incorrect understanding of facts.

2. Good faith: The person must act with due care and attention.

3. Legal belief: Under Section 14, the person must believe in good faith that they are bound by law to act. Under Section 17, they must believe in good faith that the law justifies the act.

4. No mistake of law: The protection does not apply merely because the person misunderstands the law.

5. Example

A police officer receives reliable information and, after making the required checks, arrests a person whom the officer in good faith mistakenly believes to be the person named in a lawful warrant. Depending on the circumstances, Section 14 may protect the officer if its conditions are fulfilled.

6. Importance

The defence prevents unfair punishment where a person acts in good faith because of a factual mistake. However, the belief must be honest and accompanied by due care and attention.

7. Conclusion

Mistake of fact may excuse an act when the requirements of the law are satisfied. Sections 14 and 17 of the BNS provide protection for certain acts performed in good faith under a factual mistake, while excluding mistakes of law.

20. Bigamy under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
1. Introduction

Marriage is a legally recognised relationship. Bigamy generally means marrying another person while a previous marriage is still legally valid. The law punishes certain second marriages because they violate the legal requirements of marriage.

2. Legal Provision

Section 82 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023 deals with marrying again during the lifetime of a husband or wife when the second marriage is void because the first marriage continues to subsist.

Under Section 82(1), the person may be punished with imprisonment up to seven years and a fine. Under Section 82(2), if the person conceals the former marriage from the person with whom the subsequent marriage is contracted, the punishment may extend to ten years and a fine.

3. Essential Elements

1. First valid marriage: The person must have a legally valid marriage that remains in force.

2. First spouse living: The husband or wife from the first marriage must be alive.

3. Second marriage: The person must contract another marriage.

4. Second marriage is void: The subsequent marriage must be void because of the continuing first marriage, subject to the applicable personal law and statutory exceptions.

4. Exceptions

Section 82 does not apply in certain circumstances. These include where the first marriage has been declared void by a court of competent jurisdiction, or where the former husband or wife has been continuously absent and unheard of for seven years, provided the person entering the subsequent marriage informs the other party of the true facts before the marriage.

5. Example

If A is legally married to B and, while that marriage remains valid, knowingly contracts another marriage with C that is void because of the first marriage, A may be liable for bigamy under Section 82.

6. Conclusion

Bigamy means contracting a second marriage while a legally valid first marriage continues, in circumstances covered by law. Section 82 of the BNS provides punishment and recognises specific exceptions. The validity of the marriages and the applicable personal law must be considered in each case.

28. Four persons went armed with guns to the house of A to commit robbery. One accused robbed the house, one accused caused injury to 'A', and two other accused stood outside the house to help the co-accused. Whether all the four accused are liable for the offence?

The main issue is whether all four accused are criminally liable when one commits robbery, another causes injury to A, and the remaining two stand outside the house to help them.

2. Relevant Legal Provision

Under Section 309(6) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, if a person voluntarily causes hurt while committing or attempting to commit robbery, that person and every other person jointly concerned in committing or attempting the robbery may be punished with imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years, and may also be fined.

3. Application to the Facts

In this case, all four accused went together to A's house armed with guns and with the common purpose of committing robbery. One accused robbed the house, while another caused injury to A during the robbery.

The remaining two accused stood outside to assist their companions. Their role as helpers shows that they were jointly concerned in the robbery. They cannot escape liability merely because they did not enter the house or personally take away the property.

4. Conclusion

Yes, all four accused may be held liable for robbery in which hurt was voluntarily caused, provided the prosecution proves that they were jointly concerned in committing the robbery. Section 309(6) of the BNS expressly extends liability to the other persons jointly concerned in the robbery. Since only four persons are involved, the offence is not dacoity, which requires five or more persons.

29. Assault
1. Introduction

Assault is an offence against the human body. It involves a gesture or preparation that makes another person believe that criminal force is about to be used against them. The law protects individuals from threats of immediate physical force and ensures their safety and personal liberty.

2. Definition of Assault

According to Section 130 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, a person commits assault when they make a gesture or preparation intending or knowing that it is likely to make a person present apprehend that criminal force is about to be used against them.

In simple words, assault occurs when a person's actions create a reasonable fear that physical force is about to be used against them. Actual physical contact is not necessary for assault.

3. Essential Elements of Assault

1. Gesture or Preparation: The accused must make a gesture or do something in preparation to use criminal force.

2. Intention or Knowledge: The accused must intend, or know that the action is likely, to create fear of the use of criminal force.

3. Presence of the Person: The person who is threatened must be present and capable of apprehending the threatened use of force.

4. Apprehension of Criminal Force: The gesture or preparation must cause the person to apprehend that criminal force is about to be used against them.

5. No Physical Contact Required: Assault can be complete even if the accused does not touch or injure the other person.

4. Mere Words Do Not Amount to Assault

Section 130 clearly states that mere words do not amount to assault. However, words used along with a gesture or preparation may give that action a meaning that amounts to assault.

5. Examples of Assault

Example 1: A raises a fist at B, intending to make B believe that A is about to strike B. This may amount to assault.

Example 2: A takes up a stick and threatens B, saying that A will beat B. If the gesture, understood along with the words and surrounding circumstances, causes B to apprehend the immediate use of criminal force, it may amount to assault.

Example 3: A merely threatens B verbally without making any gesture or preparation. Mere words alone do not constitute assault under Section 130.

6. Difference between Assault and Criminal Force

Assault involves a gesture or preparation that causes apprehension of the imminent use of criminal force. Criminal force, defined under Section 129 of the BNS, involves the actual use of force in the circumstances specified by law. Therefore, assault does not require physical contact, whereas criminal force involves the use of force.

7. Punishment for Assault

Under Section 131 of the BNS, 2023, assault or criminal force, except in the circumstances involving grave and sudden provocation specified by law, is punishable with imprisonment of either description for up to three months, a fine up to ₹1,000, or both. Special provisions provide different punishments for particular circumstances, such as assault to deter a public servant from performing their duty.

8. Conclusion

Assault is an offence that protects individuals from the apprehended use of criminal force. Section 130 defines assault, while Section 131 provides its general punishment. The essential point is that a gesture or preparation must create apprehension that criminal force is about to be used; actual physical injury is not necessary.

A has committed the offence of voluntarily causing grievous hurt under Section 117(1) of the BNS, 2023. Under Section 117(2), the offence is punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term that may extend to seven years, and A is also liable to a fine.

31. Rioting

Rioting is an offence against public peace and tranquillity. It occurs when members of an unlawful assembly use force or violence to achieve their common object. According to Section 191(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, whenever force or violence is used by an unlawful assembly, or by any member of it, in furtherance of the common object of that assembly, every member of the assembly is guilty of rioting.

2. Use of Force or Violence: Force or violence must actually be used by the assembly or by at least one of its members.

3. Common Object: The force or violence must be used in furtherance of the common object of the unlawful assembly.

4. Membership: Every member of the assembly may be guilty of rioting when the legal requirements are fulfilled, even if that person does not personally use force or violence.

4. Example of Rioting

Suppose six persons form an unlawful assembly to take possession of another person's land by force. During the incident, one member attacks the owner with a stick to achieve this purpose. If the legal requirements are established, the members may be guilty of rioting even though only one person directly attacks the owner.

5. Punishment for Rioting

Under Section 191(2) of the BNS, 2023, rioting is punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term that may extend to two years, a fine, or both.

Under Section 191(3), if a person commits rioting while armed with a deadly weapon, or with anything that is likely to cause death when used as a weapon of offence, the punishment may extend to five years' imprisonment, a fine, or both.

6. Difference between Unlawful Assembly and Rioting

An unlawful assembly exists when five or more persons share a common object prohibited by law. Rioting takes place when force or violence is used in furtherance of that common object. Therefore, every riot involves an unlawful assembly, but an unlawful assembly does not necessarily amount to rioting.

7. Conclusion

Rioting is a serious offence that threatens public peace and safety. Section 191 of the BNS, 2023, defines rioting and provides punishment according to the circumstances. Its essential requirement is the use of force or violence by an unlawful assembly in furtherance of its common object.

32. Unlawful Assembly
1. Introduction

An unlawful assembly is a gathering of persons whose common object is prohibited by law. Such an assembly may be formed to resist lawful authority, commit an offence, take property by force, or interfere with the rights of others. The law punishes unlawful assembly to maintain public peace and order.

2. Definition of Unlawful Assembly

According to Section 189(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, an assembly of five or more persons is an unlawful assembly if its common object falls within any of the categories specified by law.

3. Essential Elements of Unlawful Assembly

1. Assembly of Five or More Persons: The first requirement is that at least five persons must form the assembly. A gathering of fewer than five persons does not ordinarily constitute an unlawful assembly under Section 189(1).

2. Common Object: The members must share a common object that falls within one of the categories specified in Section 189(1). The common object is the purpose that the assembly intends to achieve.

3. Object Prohibited by Law: / Circumstances in Which It Becomes Unlawful The common object must fall within one of the following categories:

(a) To use or threaten criminal force to overawe the Central Government, a State Government, Parliament, a State Legislature, or a public servant exercising lawful authority.

(b) To resist the execution of any law or legal process.

(c) To commit mischief, criminal trespass, or another offence.

(d) To use or threaten criminal force to take possession of property, deprive a person of a right of way or another specified right, or enforce a right or supposed right.

(e) To use or threaten criminal force to compel a person to do something that they are not legally bound to do, or to prevent them from doing something they are legally entitled to do.

4. Knowledge and Membership: Under Section 189(2), a person who knows the facts that make an assembly unlawful and intentionally joins or continues in it is treated as a member of the unlawful assembly.

5. Change in Common Object: An assembly may become unlawful even if it was lawful at the beginning. This may happen when its common object changes into one of the prohibited objects mentioned in Section 189(1).

4. Example

If six persons gather with the common object of forcibly taking possession of another person's land by threatening the owner with criminal force, their assembly may be unlawful. The prosecution must establish the required common object and the relevant person's membership.

5. Liability of Members

Under Section 190 of the BNS, 2023, every member of an unlawful assembly may be held guilty of an offence committed by another member in furtherance of the assembly's common object, or of an offence that the members knew was likely to be committed in pursuing that object.

6. Conclusion

An unlawful assembly requires five or more persons sharing a common object prohibited by law. The number of persons, the common object, and the person's knowledge and membership are important elements. Sections 189 and 190 of the BNS, 2023, deal with unlawful assembly and the liability of its members.

33. Attempt to Murder
1. Introduction

Attempt to murder is a serious offence against human life. It occurs when a person performs an act with the intention or knowledge required for murder, but the intended death does not occur. The law punishes such conduct because the accused has taken a step towards unlawfully taking another person's life.

2. Definition of Attempt to Murder

According to Section 109(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, a person commits attempt to murder if they do an act with such intention or knowledge, and under such circumstances, that if death were caused by that act, they would be guilty of murder.

3. Essential Elements of Attempt to Murder

1. Intention or Knowledge: The accused must have the intention or knowledge required for murder. The intention may be understood from the weapon used, the part of the body targeted, the seriousness of the attack, and the surrounding circumstances.

2. An Act Towards Murder: The accused must perform an act that moves towards carrying out the intended offence. Mere preparation is generally not sufficient.

3. Circumstances of the Act: The act must be such that, if it caused death in those circumstances, the accused would be guilty of murder.

4. Death Need Not Occur: The victim may survive, and the accused may still be guilty of attempt to murder.

5. Serious Injury Is Not Essential: The victim does not necessarily have to suffer a grievous or life-threatening injury. The intention or knowledge of the accused and the circumstances of the act are important in determining the offence.

4. Examples

Example 1: A shoots at B intending to kill B, but the bullet misses. A may be guilty of attempt to murder under Section 109.

Example 2: A buys and loads a gun intending to kill B but has not yet taken a step towards carrying out the attack. This is preparation. When A fires the gun at B with the required intention or knowledge, the act amounts to attempt to murder.

Example 3: A intends to kill B by poisoning food. After mixing the poison into food, A places the food on B's table to be eaten. The act may amount to attempt to murder even if B does not eat the food.

5. Punishment for Attempt to Murder

Under Section 109(1) of the BNS, 2023, the offence is punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term that may extend to ten years, and the offender is also liable to a fine.

If hurt is caused to any person by the act, the offender may be punished with imprisonment for life or with the punishment mentioned above. Under Section 109(2), if a person already under a sentence of imprisonment for life commits this offence and causes hurt, the punishment may be death or imprisonment for life, meaning the remainder of that person's natural life.

6. Difference between Preparation and Attempt

Preparation means arranging the means required to commit an offence, such as buying a weapon. Attempt begins when the accused performs an act sufficiently connected with carrying out the offence, such as firing the weapon at the intended victim. The intention and the nature of the act are examined together.

7. Conclusion

Attempt to murder involves an act performed with the intention or knowledge required for murder, even though death does not occur. Section 109 of the BNS, 2023, defines the offence and provides punishment based on the circumstances and whether hurt is caused.

34. Affray
1. Introduction

Affray is an offence against public peace and tranquillity. It occurs when two or more persons fight in a public place and disturb public peace. Such fighting may create fear, inconvenience, and disorder among people. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, provides punishment for this offence to maintain public order and safety.

2. Definition of Affray

According to Section 194(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, when two or more persons, by fighting in a public place, disturb the public peace, they are said to commit an affray.

In simple words, affray means a fight between two or more persons in a public place that disturbs public peace.

3. Essential Elements of Affray

1. Two or More Persons: The first essential element is the involvement of at least two persons. A fight involving only one person cannot constitute affray because the law requires fighting between two or more persons.

2. Fighting: There must be actual fighting between the persons involved. A mere verbal argument, exchange of insults, or heated discussion does not by itself amount to affray unless the required fighting takes place.

3. Public Place: The fighting must occur in a public place, such as a public road, marketplace, park, or railway station. The place must be one where the public has access or may ordinarily be present.

4. Disturbance of Public Peace: The fight must disturb public peace. This is an essential requirement. A fight in a public place that does not disturb public peace does not automatically amount to affray under Section 194.

5. Participation in the Fight: The persons charged must be involved in the fighting. The prosecution must establish the facts necessary to prove that the accused participated in the conduct constituting affray.

4. Examples of Affray

Example 1: A and B start fighting physically in a crowded marketplace. Their fight creates disorder and disturbs public peace. This may amount to affray under Section 194 of the BNS.

Example 2: A and B have a heated verbal argument in a public park but do not engage in a fight. Their conduct alone does not constitute affray under Section 194.

Example 3: A and B fight inside a public bus and disturb the peace of passengers. Depending on the circumstances, their conduct may amount to affray.

5. Punishment for Affray

According to Section 194(2) of the BNS, 2023, a person who commits affray may be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term extending up to one month, or with a fine extending up to ₹1,000, or with both.

6. Difference between Affray and Rioting

Affray requires two or more persons to fight in a public place and disturb public peace. Rioting under Section 191 of the BNS requires an unlawful assembly of five or more persons and the use of force or violence in furtherance of its common object. Thus, the essential requirements of the two offences are different.

7. Conclusion

Affray is an offence intended to protect public peace and order. Under Section 194 of the BNS, 2023, its essential elements are fighting between two or more persons, a public place, and disturbance of public peace. The law provides punishment to discourage public fighting and maintain tranquillity in society.

36. Criminal Conspiracy
1. Introduction

Criminal conspiracy is an offence against law and public order. It occurs when two or more persons agree to commit an illegal act or to achieve a lawful object through illegal means. The law punishes criminal conspiracy because people may plan crimes together and work jointly to carry out their unlawful purpose.

2. Definition of Criminal Conspiracy

According to Section 61(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, when two or more persons agree with a common object to do, or cause to be done, an illegal act or an act that is not illegal by illegal means, such an agreement is called criminal conspiracy.

In simple words, criminal conspiracy means an agreement between two or more persons to do something illegal or to achieve something through illegal methods.

3. Essential Elements of Criminal Conspiracy

1. Two or More Persons: At least two persons must be involved in the agreement. A person cannot commit criminal conspiracy alone.

2. Agreement between Persons: There must be an agreement between the persons concerned. This agreement is the most important element of criminal conspiracy.

3. Common Object: The persons must agree to pursue a common object, which may involve doing an illegal act or achieving an objective through illegal means.

4. Illegal Act or Illegal Means: The agreement must relate either to an illegal act or to an act that is not illegal in itself but is to be accomplished through illegal means.

5. Further Act in Certain Cases: If the agreement is to commit an offence, the agreement itself is sufficient to constitute criminal conspiracy. However, for an agreement that does not itself involve committing an offence, at least one party must do some act in furtherance of the agreement.

4. Examples of Criminal Conspiracy

Example 1: A and B agree to rob a jewellery shop. Their agreement to commit the offence may amount to criminal conspiracy even if they have not yet carried out the robbery.

Example 2: A and B agree to obtain a lawful benefit by using illegal methods. If the conditions prescribed by Section 61 are fulfilled, their agreement may amount to criminal conspiracy.

5. Punishment for Criminal Conspiracy

Under Section 61(2)(a) of the BNS, 2023, a person who participates in a conspiracy to commit an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life, or rigorous imprisonment for two years or more may be punished in the same manner as a person who abets that offence, where the Sanhita does not expressly provide another punishment for the conspiracy.

Under Section 61(2)(b), any other criminal conspiracy is punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term up to six months, a fine, or both.

6. Conclusion

Criminal conspiracy is based on an agreement between two or more persons to pursue an illegal object or to achieve an objective through illegal means. Section 61 of the BNS, 2023, defines the offence and prescribes punishment according to the nature of the conspiracy. The agreement is its central element, and the law provides protection against the collective planning of unlawful acts.

37. When Does Culpable Homicide Amount to Murder?
1. Introduction

Culpable homicide and murder are offences against human life. Culpable homicide is defined under Section 100 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023. When culpable homicide fulfils the conditions mentioned in Section 101 and none of the statutory exceptions applies, it amounts to murder. Therefore, every murder is culpable homicide, but every culpable homicide is not murder.

2. When Culpable Homicide Amounts to Murder

According to Section 101 of the BNS, 2023, culpable homicide amounts to murder in the following four circumstances, except where one of the five exceptions applies:

1. Intention to Cause Death: If the act causing death is done with the intention of causing death, the offence amounts to murder.

Example: A intentionally shoots B to kill him, and B dies as a result. A commits murder.

2. Intention to Cause an Injury Known to Be Likely to Cause Death: If the accused intends to cause bodily injury that they know is likely to cause the death of the particular person, the offence amounts to murder.

Example: A knows that B suffers from a serious medical condition and intentionally inflicts an injury that A knows is likely to cause B's death. If B dies, the offence may amount to murder.

3. Intention to Cause a Bodily Injury Sufficient in the Ordinary Course of Nature to Cause Death: If the accused intentionally causes bodily injury that is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, the offence amounts to murder.

Example: A intentionally inflicts a sword wound on B that is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, and B dies from that injury. A commits murder even if A did not intend to cause B's death.

4. Knowledge of an Imminently Dangerous Act: If the accused knows that the act is so imminently dangerous that it will, in all probability, cause death or bodily injury likely to cause death, and performs it without any excuse for taking that risk, the offence amounts to murder.

Example: A deliberately fires a loaded cannon into a crowded area without any lawful justification, killing a person. The act may amount to murder.

3. Exceptions to Murder

Section 101 recognises five exceptions in which culpable homicide does not amount to murder, provided the conditions of the applicable exception are satisfied.

1. Grave and Sudden Provocation: If the offender loses self-control because of grave and sudden provocation and causes death, the offence may be reduced to culpable homicide not amounting to murder. This exception does not apply in the circumstances excluded by the section.

2. Exceeding the Right of Private Defence: If a person, acting in good faith in private defence, exceeds the legal power of defence and causes death without premeditation or an intention to cause more harm than necessary, the case may fall within this exception.

3. Public Servant Exceeding Powers: A public servant, or a person assisting a public servant in advancing public justice, may fall within this exception when they exceed their legal powers, act in good faith believing the act to be lawful and necessary for duty, and act without ill-will towards the deceased.

4. Sudden Fight: Culpable homicide is not murder when death occurs without premeditation during a sudden fight, in the heat of passion upon a sudden quarrel, provided the offender does not take undue advantage or act in a cruel or unusual manner.

5. Consent of the Deceased: Culpable homicide is not murder under this exception where the person whose death is caused is above eighteen years of age and voluntarily suffers death or takes the risk of death with their own consent.

4. Conclusion

Thus, culpable homicide amounts to murder under Section 101 of the BNS, 2023, when any of its four specified conditions is fulfilled and none of the five exceptions applies. The intention, knowledge, nature of the injury, circumstances of the act, and applicability of any exception are important in deciding whether the offence is murder or culpable homicide not amounting to murder.

38. Law Relating to Insanity
1. Introduction

Insanity is an important defence in criminal law. Generally, a person is held criminally responsible for an offence committed by them. However, when a person suffers from unsoundness of mind of such a nature that they cannot understand the nature of their act or that the act is wrong or contrary to law, the law may protect them from criminal liability. This defence is based on the principle that criminal responsibility requires the necessary mental understanding.

2. Legal Provision

Section 22 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023 deals with the act of a person of unsound mind. It provides that an act is not an offence if, at the time of doing it, a person, because of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that they are doing what is either wrong or contrary to law.

3. Essential Conditions of the Defence

1. Unsoundness of Mind: The accused must have been suffering from unsoundness of mind that affected their ability to understand the act. Mere unusual behaviour or a mental health diagnosis is not sufficient by itself.

2. Incapacity to Understand the Act: Due to unsoundness of mind, the accused must have been incapable of understanding the nature of the act or that the act was wrong or contrary to law, as specified in Section 22.

3. Condition at the Time of the Act: The most important question is the mental condition of the accused when the offence was committed. Previous or later mental illness may be relevant evidence, but it does not automatically establish the defence.

4. Connection with the Offence: The incapacity must result from unsoundness of mind and must satisfy the legal test given in Section 22.

4. Legal Insanity and Medical Insanity

Medical Insanity: It refers to a mental illness diagnosed from a medical point of view. A person may suffer from a mental disorder but still understand the nature and wrongfulness of their actions.

Legal Insanity: It refers to the incapacity recognised by criminal law under Section 22. The person must be incapable, because of unsoundness of mind at the relevant time, of understanding the nature of the act or that it was wrong or contrary to law.

Therefore, the court examines legal insanity and not merely the existence of a medical condition.

5. Burden of Proof

Under Section 108 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, the accused has the burden of proving the circumstances that bring the case within a general exception such as Section 22 of the BNS. The accused may rely on medical reports, expert opinions, witness statements, and conduct before, during, and after the incident. The accused is not required to prove the defence beyond reasonable doubt; the standard is generally the balance of probabilities.

6. Example

Suppose A, because of severe unsoundness of mind, is incapable of understanding the nature of an act when committing it. If the evidence establishes the requirements of Section 22, A may receive the protection of this provision. However, if A merely suffers from a mental illness but understands the nature and wrongfulness of the act, the defence will not automatically apply.

7. Conclusion

Section 22 of the BNS, 2023, provides protection to a person whose unsoundness of mind causes the specific incapacity recognised by law at the time of the act. Mere mental illness is not enough. The court must carefully examine the evidence to determine whether the legal requirements of the insanity defence are satisfied.

39. Discuss the law relating to Constructive Criminality
1. Introduction

Constructive criminality is an important principle of criminal law. Generally, a person is responsible for their own criminal acts. However, when several persons act together with a common intention or as members of an unlawful assembly with a common object, the law may make each person responsible for an offence committed by another member. This principle is known as constructive criminal liability or joint criminal liability.

2. Meaning of Constructive Criminality

Constructive criminality means holding a person criminally liable for an act committed by another person because of their shared intention or participation in a group having an unlawful common object. A person need not always perform the final criminal act personally to be held liable. However, the legal requirements of joint liability must be proved.

3. Legal Provisions under the BNS, 2023

A. Common Intention – Section 3(5)

Section 3(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, provides that when a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of them is liable for that act in the same manner as if they had done it alone.

Essential Requirements:

1. There must be two or more persons.

2. There must be a common intention shared by the accused persons.

3. A criminal act must be committed in furtherance of that common intention.

4. The accused persons must participate in the criminal act in furtherance of their shared intention. Common intention may develop at the scene, but it must exist before the criminal act is committed.

Example: A and B decide to kill C. A holds C while B stabs C, causing death. If the prosecution proves their common intention and the other legal requirements, both A and B may be liable for the offence of murder, even though only B delivered the fatal blow.

B. Common Object – Sections 189 and 190

Section 189 defines an unlawful assembly as an assembly of five or more persons whose common object falls within the categories specified by law. Section 190 provides that every member of an unlawful assembly is guilty of an offence committed by any member in prosecution of its common object, or of an offence that the members knew was likely to be committed in prosecution of that object.

Essential Requirements:

1. There must be an unlawful assembly consisting of five or more persons.

2. The assembly must have a common object prohibited by law.

3. The offence must be committed in prosecution of that common object or be an offence that the members knew was likely to be committed in pursuing it.

4. The accused must be a member of the assembly at the time the offence is committed.

Example: Five persons form an unlawful assembly to attack C. During the attack, one member causes serious injury to C in furtherance of the common object. If the requirements of Section 190 are satisfied, the other members may also be held liable for that offence.

4. Difference between Common Intention and Common Object

Common Intention: Governed by Section 3(5), it requires a shared intention and participation in the criminal act. It may involve two or more persons.

Common Object: Governed by Sections 189 and 190, it requires an unlawful assembly of at least five persons. A prior agreement or pre-planned meeting of minds is not essential, although the prohibited common object and the required basis of liability must be established.

5. Conclusion

Constructive criminality helps determine liability when people commit offences collectively. Sections 3(5) and 189–190 of the BNS, 2023, deal with joint liability based on common intention and common object. However, a person cannot be held liable merely because they are present at the scene; the legal conditions for the applicable form of liability must be proved.

40. Criminal Breach of Trust
1. Introduction

Criminal breach of trust is an offence against property. It occurs when a person is entrusted with property but dishonestly misuses it, converts it for personal use, or deals with it against the law or the agreement governing that trust. The offence involves a breach of the trust placed in a person.

2. Definition of Criminal Breach of Trust

According to Section 316(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, a person commits criminal breach of trust if, having been entrusted with property or control over property, they dishonestly misappropriate or convert it to their own use, dishonestly use or dispose of it in violation of a legal direction or contract, or wilfully allow another person to do so.

In simple words, criminal breach of trust means dishonestly misusing property that has been entrusted to a person for a particular purpose.

3. Essential Elements of Criminal Breach of Trust

1. Entrustment of Property: The property must have been entrusted to the accused. Entrustment may also involve giving the accused control or authority over the property. For example, a person may be entrusted with money to deposit in a bank.

2. Dishonest Misappropriation or Conversion: The accused must dishonestly take the property for personal use or deal with it in a manner contrary to the purpose for which it was entrusted.

3. Violation of Law or Contract: The accused may dishonestly use or dispose of the property in violation of a legal direction or an express or implied contract relating to the trust.

4. Dishonest Intention: Dishonesty is an essential requirement. An honest mistake or a genuine disagreement over the handling of property does not automatically amount to criminal breach of trust.

5. Wilfully Allowing Misuse: A person also commits the offence if they wilfully allow another person to dishonestly misuse or dispose of the entrusted property in the manner described by law.

4. Examples of Criminal Breach of Trust

Example 1: A gives ₹50,000 to B to deposit in a bank. B dishonestly keeps the money for personal use instead of depositing it. B may be guilty of criminal breach of trust.

Example 2: A warehouse keeper receives goods from B for safe storage but dishonestly sells them for personal benefit. The warehouse keeper may be guilty of criminal breach of trust.

Example 3: A receives money from B under an agreement and, in good faith, uses it differently because of an honest mistake. Without dishonest intention and the other required elements, the conduct does not automatically amount to criminal breach of trust, though it may give rise to civil liability.

5. Punishment

The BNS provides different punishments according to the position of the offender and the circumstances:

1. Section 316(2): General criminal breach of trust is punishable with imprisonment up to five years, a fine, or both.

2. Section 316(3): Where the offence is committed by a carrier, wharfinger, or warehouse keeper entrusted with the property, imprisonment may extend to seven years, and the offender is also liable to a fine.

3. Section 316(4): Where a clerk or servant commits the offence in respect of property entrusted in that capacity, imprisonment may extend to seven years, and the offender is also liable to a fine.

4. Section 316(5): Where the offence is committed by a public servant or by a banker, merchant, factor, broker, attorney, or agent in the relevant capacity, the punishment may be imprisonment for life or imprisonment up to ten years, and the offender is also liable to a fine.

6. Conclusion

Criminal breach of trust occurs when entrusted property is dishonestly misused or dealt with in violation of the law or the governing agreement. Entrustment and dishonest conduct are its essential features. Section 316 of the BNS, 2023, defines the offence and provides different punishments according to the circumstances.

41. Abetment under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
1. Introduction

Abetment is an important concept in criminal law. It means encouraging, helping, or planning with another person to do an unlawful act. A person who assists or encourages another person to commit an offence may also be held criminally liable. The law recognises abetment because a crime may be committed not only by the main offender but also with the help or encouragement of others.

2. Definition of Abetment

According to Section 45 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, a person abets the doing of a thing if they:

1. Instigate any person to do that thing;

2. Engage with one or more persons in a conspiracy to do that thing, provided an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of the conspiracy and in order to do that thing; or

3. Intentionally aid, by an act or illegal omission, the doing of that thing.

Thus, abetment can be committed in three ways: instigation, conspiracy, and intentional aid.

3. Three Ways of Abetment

1. Abetment by Instigation:

Instigation means encouraging, provoking, or urging another person to do something. It may also include deliberately making a false statement or concealing a material fact that a person is legally required to disclose, thereby causing or attempting to cause an act to be done.

Example: A repeatedly encourages B to kill C and urges B to commit the offence. A may be guilty of abetment by instigation.

2. Abetment by Conspiracy:

Under Section 45(b), abetment by conspiracy occurs when a person joins one or more other persons in a plan to do an unlawful act or another act covered by the provision. An act or illegal omission must take place in pursuance of the conspiracy and for the purpose of carrying out the plan.

Example: A and B plan to rob a house. B arranges the necessary equipment in pursuance of their plan to carry out the robbery. Depending on the circumstances, A and B may be liable for abetment by conspiracy.

3. Abetment by Intentional Aid:

Intentional aid means deliberately helping another person to commit an act. Such help may be provided by an action or by an illegal omission. Under Section 45, a person who does something before or during the commission of an act to facilitate it, and thereby actually facilitates it, is said to aid the act.

Example: A knowingly provides a weapon to B to help B commit an assault. If the legal requirements are satisfied, A may be guilty of abetment by intentional aid.

4. Important Principle

Section 46 of the BNS, 2023 explains who is an abettor. The offence of abetment may be complete even if the act abetted is not ultimately committed. For example, if A instigates B to murder C but B refuses, A may still be guilty of abetting murder.

5. Conclusion

Abetment makes a person responsible for intentionally encouraging, conspiring to bring about, or assisting in the commission of an act covered by law. Section 45 of the BNS, 2023, recognises three methods of abetment: instigation, conspiracy, and intentional aid. These provisions help ensure that people who support or encourage criminal conduct may also be held accountable under the law.
