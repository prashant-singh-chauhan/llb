Module 01 · General Principles of Crime

Constructive Liability

Law of Crimes – I · Topic 1.5

Introduction:

Constructive liability is a principle of criminal law under which a person may be held responsible for a criminal act committed by another person, even if they did not personally perform that act. It is an exception to the general rule that every person is responsible for their own acts. Constructive liability generally arises when two or more persons act together with a common intention or a common object.

Meaning of Constructive Liability:

Constructive liability means making a person legally responsible for a criminal act committed collectively. The law may hold several persons responsible for the same offence when the required legal conditions are fulfilled. However, mere presence at the scene of a crime does not automatically create criminal liability.

Essential Elements of Constructive Liability:

1. Participation of Several Persons: Generally, two or more persons must be involved in the criminal act for liability based on common intention to arise.

2. Common Intention: The persons must share a common criminal intention. Under Section 3(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS), each person may be held liable as if they had committed the criminal act themselves.

3. Common Object: When five or more persons form an unlawful assembly, its members may be held liable for an offence committed in pursuit of the assembly's common object, or for an offence they knew was likely to be committed in pursuit of that object. This principle is provided under Section 190 of the BNS.

4. Connection with the Crime: The prosecution must establish the legal requirements connecting the accused with the criminal act. The court considers the person's intention, conduct, and the surrounding circumstances.

Major Judicial Cases:
1. Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King Emperor (1925)

Facts: Barendra Kumar Ghosh and his companions went to a post office armed with firearms to rob money. During the robbery, the postmaster resisted and one of the participants shot him dead. Barendra argued that he had not fired the fatal shot.

Judgment: The Privy Council upheld Barendra's conviction for murder with the aid of Section 34 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC).

Principle of Judgment: This case established that under constructive liability based on common intention, a person may be held responsible for the criminal act of another participant. It is not necessary for every accused to perform the main criminal act personally, provided the offence was committed in furtherance of their common intention.

2. Mahbub Shah v. Emperor (1945)

Facts: A dispute arose between two groups concerning reeds near the Indus River. During the confrontation, firearms were used, and a person was killed. The prosecution sought to hold the accused responsible for murder with the aid of Section 34 IPC.

Judgment: The Privy Council rejected the inference of common intention to commit murder because the evidence did not establish a prior agreement or shared intention to commit that offence.

Principle of Judgment: The Court held that similar intention is not the same as common intention. Common intention must be established from the evidence and circumstances. Constructive liability cannot be imposed merely because persons participated in the same incident or had similar purposes.

3. Lalji v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1989)

Facts: Several accused persons were prosecuted for offences committed by members of an unlawful assembly. They challenged their convictions, including murder with the aid of Section 149 IPC, on the ground that individual criminal acts had not been established against every accused.

Judgment: The Supreme Court explained that Section 149 IPC creates constructive or vicarious criminal liability for members of an unlawful assembly when its legal requirements are satisfied.

Principle of Judgment: A member may be held liable for an offence committed in prosecution of the assembly's common object, or for an offence the members knew was likely to be committed in pursuing that object. Proof of a separate overt act by every member is not necessary, but membership and the required common object or knowledge must be established.

Example:

A and B plan to rob a shop. A stands outside to warn B if anyone approaches, while B enters the shop and steals money. If their common intention is proved, both may be held liable for the robbery, even though A did not personally take the money.

Conclusion:

Thus, constructive liability is an important principle that helps courts determine criminal responsibility in cases involving several persons. It prevents participants in a common criminal plan from escaping liability merely because one person performed the principal act. However, criminal liability must be established according to the requirements of the applicable law and the evidence of each case.
