Module 01 · General Principles of Crime

Common Intention

Law of Crimes – I · Topic 1.6

Introduction:

Common intention means a shared intention among two or more persons to commit a criminal act. It is an important principle of criminal law that helps determine the joint liability of persons involved in a crime.

Legal Provision:

Section 3(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) provides that when several persons commit a criminal act in furtherance of their common intention, each person may be held liable as if they had committed the act individually.

Essential Elements of Common Intention:

1. Two or More Persons: Common intention requires the involvement of at least two persons.

2. Shared Intention: The persons must share an intention to commit the criminal act.

3. Participation in the Crime: There must be participation in the criminal act in furtherance of the common intention.

4. Proof of Intention: Common intention may be inferred from the conduct of the accused and the surrounding circumstances.

Major Judicial Case:
1. Mahbub Shah v. Emperor (1945)

Facts: A dispute arose between two groups concerning reeds near the Indus River. During the confrontation, firearms were used, and a person was killed. The prosecution sought to hold the accused responsible for murder with the aid of Section 34 IPC.

Judgment: The Privy Council rejected the inference of common intention to commit murder because the evidence did not establish a prior agreement or shared intention to commit that offence.

Principle of Judgment: The Court held that similar intention is not the same as common intention. Common intention must be established from the evidence and circumstances. Constructive liability cannot be imposed merely because persons participated in the same incident or had similar purposes.

Example:

If A and B jointly plan a robbery and act together to carry it out, both may be held liable even if only A takes the money, provided the legal requirements are satisfied.

Conclusion:

Thus, common intention establishes joint criminal liability where the required shared intention and participation are proved. Mere presence at the crime scene is not sufficient to establish liability.
