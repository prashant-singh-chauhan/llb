Module 04 · Specific Offences

Theft Section 303

Law of Crimes – I · Topic 4.2 A

1. Theft
1. Introduction

Theft is an offence against property. It occurs when a person dishonestly takes movable property out of another person's possession without that person's consent. The main purpose of the law of theft is to protect the property and possession of individuals against unlawful taking. The offence of theft is defined under Section 303 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS).

2. Definition of Theft

According to Section 303(1) of the BNS, whoever, intending to take dishonestly any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person's consent, moves that property in order to such taking, is said to commit theft.

In simple words, theft means dishonestly taking another person's movable property without permission.

3. Essential Elements/Ingredients of Theft

1. Dishonest Intention: The person must intend to cause wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another.

2. Movable Property: The property must be movable. Land itself cannot be stolen, but things attached to the earth may become movable property when separated.

3. Property in Another Person's Possession: The property must be in the possession of another person. The offence concerns possession and does not necessarily require the person in possession to be the owner.

4. Absence of Consent: The property must be taken without the consent of the person possessing it. Consent may be express or implied.

5. Moving the Property: The accused must move the property in order to carry out the dishonest taking. Even a slight movement may be sufficient.

4. Illustrations of Theft

(a) A cuts down a tree on Z’s ground, with the intention of dishonestly taking the tree out of Z’s possession without Z’s consent. Here, as soon as A has severed the tree in order to such taking, he has committed theft.

(b) A puts a bait for dogs in his pocket, and thus induces Z’s dog to follow it. Here, if A’s intention be dishonestly to take the dog out of Z’s possession without Z’s consent. A has committed theft as soon as Z’s dog has begun to follow A.

(c) A meets a bullock carrying a box of treasure. He drives the bullock in a certain direction, in order that he may dishonestly take the treasure. As soon as the bullock begins to move, A has committed theft of the treasure.

(d) A being Z’s servant, and entrusted by Z with the care of Z’s plate, dishonestly runs away with the plate, without Z’s consent. A has committed theft.

(e) Z, going on a journey, entrusts his plate to A, the keeper of a warehouse, till Z shall return. A carries the plate to a goldsmith and sells it. Here the plate was not in Z’s possession. It could not therefore be taken out of Z’s possession, and A has not committed theft, though he may have committed criminal breach of trust.

(f) A finds a ring belonging to Z on a table in the house which Z occupies. Here the ring is in Z’s possession, and if A dishonestly removes it, A commits theft.

(g) A finds a ring lying on the highroad, not in the possession of any person. A, by taking it, commits no theft, though he may commit criminal misappropriation of property.

(h) A sees a ring belonging to Z lying on a table in Z’s house. Not venturing to misappropriate the ring immediately for fear of search and detection, A hides the ring in a place where it is highly improbable that it will ever be found by Z, with the intention of taking the ring from the hiding place and selling it when the loss is forgotten. Here A, at the time of first moving the ring, commits theft.

(i) A delivers his watch to Z, a jeweller, to be regulated. Z carries it to his shop. A, not owing to the jeweller anything for which the jeweller could lawfully detain the watch as a security, enters the shop openly, takes his watch by force out of Z’s hand, and carries it away. Here A, though he may have committed criminal trespass and assault, has not committed theft, inasmuch as what he did was not done dishonestly.

(j) If A owes money to Z for repairing the watch, and if Z retains the watch lawfully as a security for the debt, and A takes the watch out of Z’s possession with the intention of depriving Z of the security for his debt, he commits theft, inasmuch as he takes it dishonestly.

(k) Again, if A, having pawned his watch to Z, takes it out of Z’s possession without Z’s consent, not having paid what he borrowed on the watch, he commits theft, though the watch is his own property, inasmuch as he takes it dishonestly.

(l) A takes an article belonging to Z out of Z’s possession, without Z’s consent, with the intention of keeping it until he obtains a reward for its restoration. A takes dishonestly; A has therefore committed theft.

(m) A, being on friendly terms with Z, goes into Z’s library in Z’s absence, and takes away a book without Z’s express consent, for the mere purpose of reading it, and with the intention of returning it. Here, it is probable that A may have conceived that he had Z’s implied consent to use Z’s book. If this was A’s impression, A has not committed theft.

(n) A asks charity from Z’s wife. She gives A money, food and clothes, which A knows to belong to Z her husband. Here it is probable that A may conceive that Z’s wife is authorised to give away alms. If this was A’s impression, A has not committed theft.

(o) A is the paramour of Z’s wife. She gives A valuable property, which A knows to belong to her husband Z, and which A knows that she has no authority from Z to give. If A takes the property dishonestly, he commits theft.

(p) A, in good faith, believing property belonging to Z to be A’s own property, takes that property out of Z’s possession. Here, as A does not take dishonestly, he does not commit theft.

5. Punishment for Theft

Under Section 303(2) of the BNS, theft is punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three years, or with fine, or with both.

For a second or subsequent conviction under this provision, the punishment may include rigorous imprisonment for a term of not less than one year and which may extend to five years, along with fine.

In the case of a first-time offender who commits theft involving property worth less than ₹5,000 and returns the property or restores its value, the law provides for community service upon conviction.

6. Conclusion

Theft involves the dishonest taking of movable property from another person's possession without consent. Dishonest intention, movable property, possession of another person, absence of consent, and movement of the property are its essential elements. Section 303 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, provides punishment for this offence and protects individuals against the unlawful taking of their property.

2. Difference Between Theft and Extortion
1. Introduction

Theft and extortion are offences against property under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS). Theft is defined under Section 303, whereas extortion is defined under Section 308. Both offences involve dishonest intention, but they differ in the manner in which property is obtained.

2. Differences Between Theft and Extortion
Basis Theft Extortion
1. Meaning Dishonestly taking movable property from another person's possession. Dishonestly inducing a person, through fear of injury, to deliver property.
2. Force or Fear Fear or threat is not an essential element. Intentionally putting a person in fear of injury is essential.
3. Consent Property is taken without the possessor's consent. The victim delivers property because of the fear created by the offender.
4. Property It must involve movable property. It may involve property, valuable security, or certain signed or sealed documents.
5. Presence The offender need not obtain the property through the victim's presence or cooperation. The victim's delivery of property is an essential element.
6. Example A secretly steals B's mobile phone. A threatens to injure B unless B gives him money.
3. Conclusion

The main difference is that in theft, the offender dishonestly takes movable property without consent, whereas in extortion, the offender uses fear of injury to dishonestly induce the victim to deliver property. Thus, theft involves taking property, while extortion involves obtaining property through fear.

3. When Does Theft Amount to Robbery?
1. Introduction

Theft and robbery are offences against property under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS). Theft is defined under Section 303, whereas robbery is defined under Section 309. Theft is the dishonest taking of movable property without the consent of the person in possession. However, when the offender uses violence, causes hurt, or creates fear of immediate harm in connection with the theft, the offence may become robbery.

2. Legal Provision

According to Section 309(1) of the BNS, theft amounts to robbery when, in order to commit theft, while committing theft, or while carrying away or attempting to carry away property obtained by theft, the offender voluntarily causes or attempts to cause death, hurt, or wrongful restraint, or fear of instant death, instant hurt, or instant wrongful restraint.

3. Essential Conditions

1. Commission or Attempt to Commit Theft: The act must involve committing theft or attempting to commit theft.

2. Use of Violence or Fear: The offender must voluntarily cause or attempt to cause death, hurt, or wrongful restraint, or create fear of instant death, instant hurt, or instant wrongful restraint.

3. Connection with Theft: The violence or fear must occur in order to commit the theft, while committing it, or while carrying away or attempting to carry away the stolen property.

4. Immediate Nature of Fear: Where the offence involves fear, the fear must relate to instant death, instant hurt, or instant wrongful restraint. A general threat of future harm does not, by itself, satisfy this requirement.

4. Illustrations

Illustration 1: A secretly takes B's wallet. When B tries to stop him, A punches B and escapes with the wallet. If the statutory conditions are fulfilled, A commits robbery.

Illustration 2: A points a weapon at B and threatens to hurt B immediately unless B allows A to take B's mobile phone. If the threat creates fear of instant hurt and the other statutory requirements are satisfied, the offence amounts to robbery.

Illustration 3: A steals a bag and, while escaping with it, wrongfully restrains B to prevent B from stopping him. If the legal requirements of Section 309 are fulfilled, A commits robbery.

5. Difference Between Theft and Robbery

Theft involves dishonestly taking movable property without consent. Robbery involves theft accompanied by the specified violence, attempt to cause harm, wrongful restraint, or fear of immediate harm connected with the theft. Therefore, robbery is a more serious form of theft because it involves an additional element of violence or immediate danger.

6. Conclusion

Theft amounts to robbery when the offender voluntarily causes or attempts to cause death, hurt, or wrongful restraint, or fear of instant death, instant hurt, or instant wrongful restraint, in connection with committing the theft or carrying away the stolen property. Thus, the decisive factor is the presence of the additional elements specified under Section 309(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

4. Can a Person Commit Theft of His Own Property?
1. Introduction

Yes, a person can commit theft of his own property in certain circumstances. Generally, we think that a person cannot steal his own property. However, under criminal law, theft is concerned not only with ownership but also with the possession of property. Under Section 303 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS), theft means dishonestly taking movable property out of another person's possession without that person's consent.

2. Explanation

A person may own a property, but another person may have its lawful possession. If the owner dishonestly takes that property away without the possessor's consent, the act may amount to theft.

For example, A borrows money from B and pledges his gold watch to B as security for the loan. Before repaying the loan, A secretly takes the watch from B without B's permission, intending to deprive B of the security. In this situation, A may commit theft even though the watch belongs to him.

3. Another Example

A gives his watch to a jeweller for repair. The jeweller lawfully keeps the watch until A pays the repair charges. If A dishonestly takes the watch without permission to defeat the jeweller's lawful right to retain it, A may commit theft, provided all the legal requirements are satisfied.

4. Important Legal Principle

The main point is that theft depends on dishonest taking from another person's possession, not merely on who owns the property. However, the accused must have dishonest intention, the property must be movable, it must be in another person's possession, it must be taken without consent, and it must be moved to carry out the dishonest taking.

5. Conclusion

Therefore, a person can commit theft of his own property if it is in another person's lawful possession and he dishonestly takes it away without consent. Ownership alone is not a complete defence. However, if there is no dishonest intention or another essential element of theft is missing, the act will not amount to theft.

5. Difference Between Theft and Misappropriation of Property
1. Introduction

Theft and dishonest misappropriation of property are offences under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS). Theft is defined under Section 303, while dishonest misappropriation of property is covered under Section 314. Both offences involve dishonest intention, but they differ in the manner in which the property is taken or used.

2. Differences Between Theft and Misappropriation
Basis Theft Misappropriation
1. Meaning Dishonestly taking movable property from another person's possession without consent. Dishonestly using or converting movable property for one's own use.
2. Initial Possession The property is in another person's possession when it is taken. The property may initially come into the person's possession lawfully or innocently.
3. Consent The property is taken without the possessor's consent. The property may initially be received with consent or found accidentally.
4. Dishonest Intention Dishonest intention exists when the property is taken. Dishonest intention may develop after the property comes into possession.
5. Example A secretly takes B's mobile phone. A finds B's wallet and dishonestly keeps the money for personal use.
3. Conclusion

The main difference is that theft involves dishonestly taking property from another person's possession, whereas misappropriation involves dishonestly converting property to one's own use. In theft, dishonest intention exists at the time of taking; in misappropriation, it may arise later.

6. Define Theft, Robbery and Dacoity with Illustrations and Distinguish Between Them
1. Introduction

Theft, robbery and dacoity are offences against property under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS). Theft is defined under Section 303, robbery under Section 309, and dacoity under Section 310. These offences differ mainly in the use of violence and the number of persons involved.

2. Theft: Section 303 BNS

Definition: A person commits theft when, intending to take movable property dishonestly from another person's possession without consent, the person moves that property to carry out the taking.

Illustration: A secretly takes B's mobile phone from a table without permission, intending to keep it. A commits theft.

Essential elements: Dishonest intention, movable property, possession of another person, absence of consent, and movement of the property.

3. Robbery: Section 309 BNS

Definition: Robbery is an aggravated form of theft or extortion. Theft amounts to robbery when, in order to commit theft, while committing it, or while carrying away or attempting to carry away the stolen property, the offender voluntarily causes or attempts to cause death, hurt or wrongful restraint, or fear of instant death, instant hurt or instant wrongful restraint.

Extortion amounts to robbery when the offender is present and puts the victim in fear of instant death, instant hurt or instant wrongful restraint, thereby inducing the victim to deliver property then and there.

Illustration: A steals B's wallet. When B tries to stop him, A strikes B and escapes with the wallet. If the statutory conditions are fulfilled, A commits robbery.

4. Dacoity: Section 310 BNS

Definition: When five or more persons conjointly commit or attempt to commit robbery, or when the total number of persons committing or attempting robbery together with those present and aiding them is five or more, every person involved in that manner commits dacoity.

Illustration: Five persons jointly threaten a shopkeeper, use immediate violence as required by law, and take money from the shop. If the legal requirements of robbery are satisfied, their act amounts to dacoity.

5. Difference Between Theft, Robbery and Dacoity
Basis Theft Robbery Dacoity
Meaning Dishonest taking of movable property without consent. Theft or extortion accompanied by the additional conditions specified by law. Robbery committed or attempted by five or more persons acting together as prescribed by law.
Violence Violence is not essential. Violence, restraint or fear of immediate harm is involved as specified by law. Involves robbery and the required group participation.
Number of Persons One or more persons. One or more persons. At least five persons, including those present and aiding, as specified by law.
Consent Property is taken without consent. Property is taken or delivered under the circumstances specified for robbery. Robbery is committed or attempted jointly by the required number of persons.
Example A secretly takes B's phone. A threatens immediate harm and takes B's wallet. Five persons jointly commit a robbery.
6. Conclusion

Theft involves dishonest taking of movable property without consent. Robbery is a more serious offence because it involves violence, restraint or fear of immediate harm under the conditions prescribed by law. Dacoity is robbery committed or attempted by five or more persons acting together as defined under Section 310 of the BNS. Thus, the nature of the act, the use of violence and the number of persons distinguish these three offences.
