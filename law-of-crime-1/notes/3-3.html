Module 03 · General Exceptions

Necessity

Law of Crimes – I · Topic 3.3

Introduction:

Necessity is an important general exception under criminal law. It means performing an act that may cause some harm to prevent a greater or more serious harm. Sometimes, a person must choose between two harmful situations to protect human life or property. In such circumstances, the law may excuse the act if the legal requirements are fulfilled.

Legal Provision – Section 19, BNS, 2023:

Section 19 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS), deals with an act likely to cause harm but done without criminal intention and to prevent other harm. It provides that an act is not an offence merely because the person knows it is likely to cause harm, provided the act is done without criminal intention to cause harm and in good faith to prevent or avoid other harm to a person or property. It corresponds to Section 81 of the former Indian Penal Code, 1860.

Essential Elements of Necessity:

1. Prevention of Greater Harm: The act must be performed to prevent or avoid another harm to a person or property.

2. Absence of Criminal Intention: The person must not have a criminal intention to cause the harm resulting from the act.

3. Good Faith: The act must be done honestly for the purpose of preventing or avoiding harm.

4. Imminent Danger: The danger must be sufficiently serious and immediate to justify the risk taken. The court decides this question according to the facts of each case.

5. Reasonable Choice: The nature of the danger and the harm likely to be caused must be considered when deciding whether the act is justified.

Illustrations:

1. Fire Example: During a major fire, A pulls down a house to prevent the fire from spreading to nearby houses and saving human lives. If A acts in good faith and the danger is sufficiently serious and immediate, A may be protected under Section 19.

2. Ship Example: A ship captain must change the ship's course to avoid hitting a boat carrying many passengers. The new course creates a risk of hitting a smaller boat. If the captain acts in good faith to prevent the greater danger, the defence may apply if the statutory conditions are fulfilled.

Important Case Law: R v. Dudley and Stephens (1884)

Facts: Four sailors were stranded at sea after a shipwreck. After many days without food, Dudley and Stephens killed a young cabin boy, Richard Parker, and ate his body to survive.

Judgment: The English court held Dudley and Stephens guilty of murder and rejected necessity as a defence to the intentional killing of an innocent person.

Principle of Judgment: The case demonstrates that necessity does not automatically justify every harmful act. In particular, deliberately killing an innocent person cannot be justified merely by claiming that doing so would save others.

Conclusion:

Thus, necessity protects a person who causes a risk of harm in good faith to prevent a greater and sufficiently imminent danger, when the requirements of Section 19 BNS are satisfied. However, the defence is not unlimited, and the court examines the circumstances of each case before granting protection.
